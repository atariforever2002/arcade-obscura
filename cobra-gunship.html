Meadows Games, 1976

Website Snapshot

Cobra Gunship is one of those machines that makes 1976 look more adventurous than the usual history-book version of early video games. Meadows Games built a first-person gunship combat machine around a dedicated flight yoke, a recessed black-and-white display, physical cockpit overlays, scaling targets, a banking horizon, sound effects, and a cabinet intended to make the player feel as if he were looking through an aircraft instrument panel rather than standing at an ordinary television game. Even more unusually, it accomplished this without a conventional microprocessor: the game belongs to the late discrete-logic era, with TTL circuitry doing the work later games would hand to software.

Today Cobra Gunship is extremely difficult to experience properly. Original cabinets are scarce, MAME preserves its ROM/PROM data but does not emulate the TTL circuitry into a playable game, and several important operating documents remain outside easy public access. Yet enough factory material, period trade reporting, preserved hardware, ROM/PROM data, and authentic survivor footage remain to reconstruct a surprisingly rich picture of the game—and to correct a long-repeated design credit along the way.

Technical snapshot

  • Meadows Games · 1976 · Model CG-1
  • Dedicated upright · one or two players, sequential
  • Black-and-white raster CRT with physical cockpit overlays
  • Specialized flight yoke with built-in firing control
  • Discrete TTL circuitry; no conventional CPU
  • Factory dimensions: approximately 66 × 28 × 33 inches (H × W × D)
  • MAME preservation status as checked September 12, 2026: not working, no emulated sound

A COCKPIT IN THE ARCADE

There are arcade games that matter because they sold in enormous numbers, introduced famous characters, or established genres. Cobra Gunship matters for a different reason. It looks like a machine from a slightly stranger branch of video-game history, one in which the arcade industry was already reaching toward simulation while much of its technology still belonged to the age of Pong.

Released by Meadows Games in 1976, with July the safest documented introduction period, Cobra Gunship was an ambitious attempt to put the player behind the controls of an attack aircraft. It was not merely a shooting game decorated with military artwork. Meadows built the cabinet, controls and display around the sensation of piloting.

The player faced a deeply recessed black-and-white monitor and took hold of the machine's defining feature: a purpose-built flight yoke. Pushing and pulling controlled climb and dive, while turning the yoke banked the aircraft. The firing control was built into the yoke, so aiming, maneuvering and shooting remained part of the same physical action. Meadows also promoted the control system's magnetic reed switches—sealed switching devices intended to reduce the mechanical contact problems an operator might otherwise have to service.

That specialized control mattered. Joysticks were becoming familiar by 1976, but Meadows did not want Cobra Gunship to feel like another abstract video game being steered around a screen. The player was supposed to fly it.

The cabinet reinforced the idea before a coin was inserted. Meadows advertised dimensions of approximately 66 inches high, 28 inches wide and 33 inches deep. The upper monitor enclosure projected outward and surrounded the display, visually isolating the battlefield from the room around it. The control area sat beneath it like a compact instrument station. It was not a full sit-down simulator, but it borrowed the language of one.

THE BATTLEFIELD

The basic objective is easy to understand: maneuver the gunship, bring enemy targets into the sight, and destroy them.

The presentation was unusually ambitious for its time. Meadows identified jeeps, tanks, half-tracks, helicopter transports and enemy gunships among the targets. Factory advertising describes distant targets beginning as specks and increasing in size as the player closes on them. The horizon reacts as the aircraft climbs, dives and banks. Together those effects produce an impression of depth and forward movement that would have looked striking in a mid-1970s arcade.

This is important historically, but it should not be exaggerated. Cobra Gunship has sometimes been mentioned in discussions of extremely early first-person scrolling games. The surviving evidence proves perspective effects, changing target scale and a responsive horizon. It does not yet prove exactly how the original video circuitry created all movement or that the machine meets a rigorous technical definition of scrolling. Nor does it justify declaring Cobra Gunship the first of anything without a separate comparison against other early first-person games such as Kee/Atari's Pursuit and Taito's Interceptor.

The game is impressive enough without manufacturing a record for it.

THE CABINET WAS PART OF THE PICTURE

A raw video signal does not completely reproduce what a Cobra Gunship player saw.

Surviving hardware and authentic restoration footage show that the cabinet itself supplied part of the visual system. A physical gunsight/crosshair was applied at the display rather than simply generated as another electronic object. Survivor reports also document transparent labels and indicator material around the monitor, including GAME OVER, ONE PLAYER and TWO PLAYER markings, along with a red CRASH lens on at least one surviving cabinet.

That combination of electronic video and physical presentation was entirely at home in the early arcade industry. Designers routinely mixed television imagery with overlays, artwork, lamps, mirrors and other cabinet effects when the electronics alone could not economically provide the complete illusion.

In Cobra Gunship, the technique made the screen feel more like something viewed through a cockpit than an exposed black-and-white television.

Period Meadows advertising even called attention to cabinet serviceability. The upper display assembly could be opened to give an operator access to the television monitor and internal components. This was still coin-operated equipment, after all: a convincing fantasy on the front side had to remain a maintainable business machine from the back.

FLYING WITHIN THE LIMITS

The yoke does more than move a gunsight. The aircraft has flight limits.

Period documentation says that climbing too high or diving too low can trigger a CRASH condition. The important confirmed rule is that a crash carries no scoring penalty. Contemporary descriptions characterize CRASH as a momentary indication, while surviving physical hardware includes a red CRASH lens. Exactly how the indicator, overlay and video circuitry interact is not completely documented in the public material, so the finished profile should not pretend that every detail of the effect has been reconstructed.

The precise recovery sequence after a crash also remains unresolved. It is not yet safe to state whether the game pauses, re-centers the aircraft, resets some target state or simply resumes after the warning. What can be said confidently is that the player can exceed the permitted flight envelope, the machine announces the crash, and the score is not reduced.

That turns aggressive maneuvering into a small risk-management problem. The player wants to line up targets quickly without simply throwing the aircraft against its limits.

SCORE MAKES THE WAR FASTER

One of the clearest surviving gameplay rules is Cobra Gunship's score-based difficulty curve.

Target speed increases when the player reaches 250 points. It increases again at 500, 750 and 1,000 points.

That gives the score a direct effect on play. Success does not merely produce a larger number; it makes the battlefield harder to control. A player who survives long enough to accumulate a strong score gets less time to acquire, track and engage targets.

The individual point values for the different vehicles have not been recovered with enough confidence to publish. Authentic survivor footage includes an owner reporting that he reached 70 points during a demonstration. That is useful evidence that the scoring system was operating on the surviving machine, but it is only a total score. It does not mean that any specific target was worth 70 points.

This is one of several places where restraint matters. A neat scoring table would be nice to print, but an invented one would make the profile worse, not better.

THE MYSTERY OF THE MISSES

The most interesting unresolved gameplay question is something an arcade operator normally needed to understand immediately: what ends the game?

Contemporary Play Meter coverage from July 1976 describes game length in terms of an operator-adjustable number of misses. That article presents the misses setting as a normal operating feature of the machine and describes selectable values rather than a fixed timed session.

Surviving Meadows factory paperwork complicates the story dramatically.

On an early information sheet, misses-related setting material has been visibly crossed out and replaced by the handwritten notation 2.2 MINUTES. Nearby text still contains older references to misses. The document looks less like a clean final specification than paperwork caught while the rules were changing.

This is unusually valuable historical evidence precisely because it is messy. Rather than two modern websites disagreeing about an old game, we appear to have contemporary documentation preserving two rule states close to the game's release.

What we cannot yet determine is the chronology of the change in production. Meadows may have converted the game from misses-based play to a fixed 2.2-minute game before most cabinets shipped. Early and later configurations may both have reached operators. The handwritten change may represent an engineering or field revision with narrower distribution. The surviving public evidence does not settle it.

Even the exact definition of a MISS remains uncertain. It should not automatically be interpreted as every fired shot that failed to hit a target; the complete final manual's Game Sequence and Adjustments sections would be much stronger evidence than guesswork from ordinary shooter conventions.

For now the contradiction belongs in the history rather than being edited out of it.

ONE PILOT AT A TIME

Cobra Gunship supports one or two players, but it is not a two-person cooperative aircraft.

Two-player operation is sequential. Players take turns using the same cockpit and controls, competing through their scores. The arrangement fits the physical design: everything about the machine is centered on one yoke and one pilot's view.

Meadows' own flyer describes the previous player's score being flashed so the next player knows what he has to beat. It is a small detail, but it reinforces how the cabinet turned a single-player simulator idea into an arcade competition without redesigning the controls for two simultaneous pilots.

SOUND IN A BLACK-AND-WHITE COCKPIT

Cobra Gunship was not silent. Meadows advertised realistic graphics and sound effects, and contemporary reporting specifically mentions sound accompanying flight action such as climbing and diving. Early factory service material also identifies a separate sound board.

The exact catalog of sound effects has not been documented well enough in the surviving public sources to write a detailed audio breakdown. That uncertainty matters because current MAME does not emulate the machine into a working audiovisual game. The appropriate conclusion is simply that sound was part of the original cockpit presentation, even though a modern emulator cannot yet demonstrate it accurately for us.

HINTS FROM THE ARCADE

Detailed high-level strategy is difficult to reconstruct for a game that almost nobody can presently play under known-correct conditions, but the confirmed rules still suggest useful principles.

Smooth yoke movement is likely more valuable than constant wild maneuvering. Climb and dive limits are real, and crossing them can produce a crash. Positioning the aircraft and aiming should also be thought of as one action: Meadows designed the control so the player could maneuver and fire without shifting to a separate panel button.

Target acquisition becomes increasingly important as the score rises. The known speed jumps at 250, 500, 750 and 1,000 points mean that comfortable early-game habits become less forgiving as the player succeeds.

Beyond that, target priorities and score-maximizing tactics should remain open until a fully documented survivor or accurate circuit reconstruction can be tested. Cobra Gunship does not need imaginary secret techniques added fifty years later.

A GAME WITHOUT A CPU

The electronics are one of the most fascinating things about Cobra Gunship.

There is no conventional microprocessor executing a game program. The machine belongs to the discrete TTL era. Networks of logic devices, counters, timing components and related circuitry collectively perform jobs that later arcade boards would assign to software.

Cobra Gunship does contain ROM and PROM devices, and eleven preserved devices are cataloged in the current MAME set. But calling them ordinary program ROMs would be misleading. The surrounding hardware circuit is effectively the program; the ROMs and PROMs provide stored patterns, lookup information and other data used by that circuitry.

That distinction changes how the game has to be preserved and reverse-engineered. With a CPU-driven title, program code can often reveal scoring rules, timers, object behavior and state transitions. Here, a crucial part of the behavior exists in the physical connectivity among components. Dumped ROMs are valuable pieces of the machine, but they are not the whole machine.

Targeted examination of the preserved data found obvious internal structure. Several of the 512-byte devices contain repeated or closely related patterns, while the small 32-byte PROMs contain deliberate compact table-like data. One device, cobra.m6, is particularly regular and geometric. Those observations are technically interesting, but they do not prove exact circuit roles. In particular, m6 should not be labeled the “horizon ROM” without schematics or circuit tracing that demonstrates the connection.

Naively drawing the ROM contents as raster graphics also produces strange fragmented patterns. That has helped generate past concern that the dumps might be corrupt. The targeted audit found no simple smoking-gun corruption signature, and unknown physical address/output mapping provides an entirely plausible explanation for graphics that look wrong when treated as ordinary linear bitmap data.

The responsible position is therefore not “the dumps are bad.” It is “the circuitry needed to interpret them correctly is still incompletely reconstructed.”

MAME PRESERVES THE PIECES, NOT THE GAMEPLAY

Cobra Gunship is cataloged in MAME under the short name cgunship, but the presence of an entry should not be mistaken for playable emulation.

Current official MAME source preserves the known ROM/PROM inventory, while the actual Meadows TTL netlist remains essentially a placeholder. The source itself warns that its timing values were copied from Pong and are not accurate for this driver. Cobra Gunship remains flagged MACHINE_NO_SOUND | MACHINE_NOT_WORKING.

In plain English: MAME knows the machine exists and preserves important digital components from it, but it does not currently reproduce the original game circuitry as a functioning game.

That makes Cobra Gunship a particularly good example of the limits of ROM preservation. Saving the contents of every programmable device is necessary, but on a CPU-less TTL machine it may still be insufficient to bring the game back digitally.

Full circuit schematics are known to survive in collector hands. A future preservation effort that combines those schematics with original hardware measurements and the preserved ROM/PROM data could potentially move the machine from cataloged artifact to genuine emulation.

SURVIVORS AND THE VIEW FROM 2019

Authentic modern footage provides a rare bridge between the paperwork and the physical machine.

The Reality Bin's 2019 Cobra Gunship Project videos document an original 1976 Meadows cabinet rather than a software recreation. The first video establishes the survivor and shows the physical construction, including an overlay that had partly come loose during transportation. The owner specifically explains that the crosshair is on the monitor/display assembly rather than being generated as part of the raw picture.

The machine was working imperfectly, and filming a black-and-white CRT introduces severe strobing and exposure problems. That makes the videos poor evidence for fine timing or subtle target behavior. They are nevertheless extremely valuable for understanding how much of the player's experience belonged to the cabinet rather than the electronic video signal alone.

Older collector documentation adds several hardware details. One 2005 survivor was reported with a 19-inch Motorola black-and-white monitor, model XM501-10 / chassis 19VP111, and an 8.079 MHz crystal on the game board. Those are authentic survivor observations, but without complete factory schematics they should not automatically be treated as universal specifications for every production cabinet.

Collector discussions also describe more than one board configuration, including a variant with additional jumpers or daughterboard hardware. It is tempting to connect such differences to the documented rules revision, but no evidence presently proves that relationship. “Different board revision exists” is evidence. “The daughterboard created 2.2-minute play” is still a hypothesis.

WHO ACTUALLY DEVELOPED COBRA GUNSHIP?

One of the most useful results of this research is the correction of a widely repeated creator credit.

Atari co-founder Ted Dabney worked with Meadows and has often been identified in modern databases and histories as the designer of Cobra Gunship. Dabney himself contradicted that claim.

During his Smithsonian oral-history interview, he was specifically told that he had received credit for Cobra Gunship and asked whether he remembered developing it. Dabney said he had not developed the game. He remembered helping the actual developer extensively but could no longer remember the man's name.

A much stronger candidate appears in the professional history of C.W. “Bill” Hicks. Hicks lists Cobra Gunship among his Meadows black-and-white arcade projects and describes his role for that group as sole engineer/programmer responsible for hardware and software designs.

The best current attribution is therefore careful but meaningful: C.W. “Bill” Hicks is strongly evidenced as the principal engineer/developer of Cobra Gunship. Ted Dabney substantially assisted the project but explicitly denied being its developer.

A contemporary Meadows document independently naming Hicks has not yet surfaced. That missing period corroboration is worth acknowledging, but it does not justify continuing to repeat a Dabney credit that Dabney himself rejected.

NOT A FLOP JUST BECAUSE IT IS RARE

Very few Cobra Gunship cabinets are encountered today, but present-day rarity should not be rewritten into a story of immediate commercial failure.

Period trade reporting tells a different story. In September 1976, Cash Box described Cobra Gunship as a “big seller for Meadows.” Other contemporary distributor reports described strong interest and placed it among hot current products. The machine continued to appear in active-trade and distributor material into 1977 and 1978.

Trade magazines naturally contained promotion and boosterism, so this is not proof of a giant production run. No trustworthy factory production quantity has been recovered. Nor has a reliable original 1976 factory selling price been established. Later distributor asking prices—such as $995 advertisements appearing well after introduction—are useful evidence that the machine remained in commercial circulation, but they should not be mislabeled as Meadows' launch MSRP.

The safe conclusion is narrower and more interesting: Cobra Gunship drew real operator/distributor attention and appears to have sold strongly enough to be discussed positively in the trade. Its survival rate half a century later is a separate question.

COLLECTOR'S NOTE

A surviving Cobra Gunship is more than a rare cabinet. It preserves a technological branch of arcade history that is unusually difficult to recreate once the original hardware disappears.

The monitor, physical overlay and indicators, specialized yoke, sound circuitry, artwork and TTL logic board all contribute to the authentic experience. Replacing the display and reproducing only a rough video output would preserve only part of what Meadows built.

Fortunately, the documentation trail is better than the machine's rarity might suggest. A final Meadows Model CG-1 operator manual is known to survive. A currently documented new-old-stock copy shows that its contents include sections titled Game Sequence and Adjustments—exactly the portions most likely to resolve the remaining questions about misses, timing and post-crash behavior. Andy Welburn's arcade manual collection also lists the Model CG-1 operator manual and notes that his copy does not include schematics.

Full logic schematics are nevertheless known from separate collector holdings. Kurz-Kasch later supported Cobra Gunship with TF-650 diagnostic program card 220ABC, another sign that service information persisted after the game's commercial life.

Those materials are not necessarily lost. They are scattered.

That is an encouraging preservation problem. A clean scan of the final manual and complete schematics could answer several historical questions at once and, more importantly, give a circuit-level emulator far better evidence than blind interpretation of the ROM/PROM files.

RESEARCH NOTE: THE QUESTIONS WE ARE LEAVING OPEN

Several gaps remain intentionally visible because the evidence does not justify closing them.

The production game-duration rule remains unresolved. July 1976 trade reporting describes operator-adjustable misses, while Meadows factory paperwork visibly changes the setting to 2.2 minutes. We do not know exactly when that revision occurred or which cabinets received which configuration.

The exact definition of a MISS is unknown.

Individual point values for the jeep, tank, half-track, helicopter transport and gunship targets have not been established reliably.

The complete sequence after a CRASH is uncertain. No scoring penalty is confirmed, but the precise reset, pause or recovery behavior is not.

A factory production total and original 1976 factory selling price remain unknown.

C.W. Hicks has strong first-person professional evidence connecting him to the game as principal engineer/developer, but a recovered 1976 Meadows credit naming him has not been found.

True technical scrolling has not been demonstrated, so no “first scrolling game” claim should be published.

Full logic schematics and the final operator manual are known to survive but were not recovered in complete public form during this research project.

These are not holes to disguise. They define the boundary between what survives and what we would merely like to know.

WHY COBRA GUNSHIP MATTERS

Cobra Gunship did not create a franchise. It did not introduce a mascot. Meadows itself disappeared, the game became scarce, and modern emulation has not yet restored it to easy play.

Yet the machine captures an important moment in arcade design.

By 1976, designers were discovering that a video game did not have to present abstract objects on an exposed screen. A cabinet could try to place the player somewhere else. Specialized controls could reinforce the illusion. Electronic graphics, physical overlays, lamps, sound and artwork could function as one experience rather than as separate decorations.

Cobra Gunship pushed hard in that direction using technology descended directly from the earliest commercial video games.

Its circuitry belongs to the discrete-logic era.

Its ambitions point toward the simulator.

That tension is what makes it fascinating.

Stand in front of the original cabinet and the idea is immediate: grab the yoke, look through the gunsight, bank the aircraft, watch targets grow as they approach, fire, keep the gunship inside its flight limits, and try to stay ahead of a battlefield that becomes faster as the score climbs.

For a few minutes in a 1976 arcade, Meadows was trying to make the television disappear and leave the player in the cockpit.

Nearly fifty years later, enough evidence survives for us to see just how ambitious that was.
