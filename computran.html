The company between the game and the arcade floor

Computran Industries is easiest to recognize through its machines. A tank game promoted under license from a West German company, a dockworker game with a Taito licensing line on its marquee, and a maze game whose company name is tucked into an illustrated glove form the securely documented core. Around them are woodgrain cabinets, frog artwork, derivative games and collector accounts that suggest a larger operation without supplying a complete production ledger.

Computran survives in preserved game programs, private-collection cabinets, period advertising and trade directories. Its American commercial identity appears in the cabinet graphics and sales literature, while software credits connect several titles to other suppliers. The contracts joining those parts of the business are largely absent.

Computran worked between other game suppliers and American operators. For Super Tank, it explicitly presented itself as the licensed manufacturer. Port Man and Triple Punch also received company-branded artwork, although their complete commercial arrangements remain unknown. These products place Computran in the manufacturing, licensing and presentation business; none of the three has been established as an original Computran software development.

Computran was publicly associated with Pasquale “Pat” Storino, an experienced coin-op operator whose amusement background preceded the company. Its Point Pleasant Beach address connected it to an established boardwalk business environment. This was a venture entering the video-game market from the operating side of the amusement industry, rather than a company that can presently be traced from a documented programmer's workshop to a series of original games.

Its short visible history also crosses several different markets. Super Tank supplies a clear example of declared international licensing. Port Man suggests experimentation with a licensed product whose American rollout may have been extremely limited. Triple Punch shows how a title could acquire different commercial identities through its artwork and supplier channels. Bull Frog and Amigo raise hardware and attribution questions that dedicated cabinets alone cannot settle.

In 1982 Nintendo named Computran in reporting about its New Jersey injunction campaign. The report does not identify the company's machines or supply a final judgment. SMS Manufacturing also began operating with Storino participation during that year, while Computran remained in trade listings into 1983. The two company names overlapped.

An operator's background

Pat Storino already knew the coin-operated amusement business when Computran appeared. His earlier career connected him with S&S Amusement and with regional and national operator activity well before the video-game venture. Later Point Pleasant Beach interests placed him within the Jenkinson's boardwalk amusement environment. Computran entered an existing network of amusement locations, maintenance work and supplier relationships.

That background helps explain the kind of business Computran became. An operator's experience concerns the complete machine: how players recognize it, whether the controls feel right, how reliably it runs, how quickly a fault can be repaired and whether its earnings justify its floor space. Those concerns differ from writing the original game program, but they are part of bringing a commercial arcade product into service.

Computran's intended production model, financing and share ownership remain unknown. Storino's operating experience preceded the venture, but the surviving company records do not identify the investors behind each product or the contractor responsible for its wooden cabinets.

The public leadership evidence is clearer. A Play Meter directory identifies Pat Storino as president and Reggie Alexander as engineering director. It gives the product line simply as video games. That entry places Computran within the industry's manufacturer directory and establishes two named responsibilities, without describing the size of the staff or the work performed by the engineering department.

The Computran entry carries an erroneous March 1, 1981 footer. The cover and issue identification place it in the March 1, 1982 Directory Issue, Volume 8, Number 5. Pat Storino's presidency and Reggie Alexander's engineering-director position are therefore documented in 1982, rather than a year earlier.

The directory identifies Alexander as engineering director without naming a program or circuit board he designed. His individual responsibilities and later career remain unknown. Computran's licensed-product documentation covers wiring, configuration and installation, but it does not allocate those tasks to named employees.

Computran's engineering department existed alongside its use of externally supplied games. Super Tank was advertised under a foreign license, and the other marked titles had separate software or commercial credits. The department's exact division of responsibilities remains unknown.

Point Pleasant Beach and the technical address

Computran's best-established public address is 3 Broadway, with P.O. Box 1872, Point Pleasant Beach, New Jersey 08742. Its advertising and directory material use telephone 201-295-2777. Super Tank promotional material also supplies telex 132168. Together these details form a consistent commercial identity rather than a name that appears only in modern game databases.

The Broadway address sat within the Storino and Jenkinson's amusement-property context. It was a place where the company could be contacted; the evidence does not yet show exactly what work occurred there. Calling it Computran's factory would go beyond the record. Office administration, storage, servicing or assembly may have taken place within the wider operation, but the allocation of space has not been established.

A second address appears in the Super Tank technical documentation: RD-2 Jackson Road, Vincentown, New Jersey 08088, accompanied by telephone 609-268-8044. This is a documented technical contact, not a verified second factory. Its relationship to the Broadway address has not been recovered from a contemporary company explanation.

The Vincentown contact uses a rural-route address rather than the Broadway street address. Its premises have not been identified in contemporary company records. The designation leaves open whether the recipient occupied a workshop, residence, warehouse or contractor's facility; it does not establish a second factory.

Super Tank's technical documentation covered installation and service as well as the game itself. Computran maintained separate commercial and technical contact addresses, but their respective responsibilities are unknown: the surviving material does not allocate assembly, servicing or customer support between Broadway and Vincentown.

The supplier of Computran's wooden cabinets has not been identified. Surviving machines preserve their construction, hardware mounting and title-specific graphics, but no known woodshop invoice or cabinet contract ties those features to a named manufacturer. Whether Computran commissioned shells, purchased finished enclosures or built them within its own operation remains unresolved.

Super Tank: the clearest product relationship

Super Tank is the anchor of the Computran story because the company states its role in its own advertising. The preserved two-page flyer identifies Computran as manufacturing the game under license from Video Games GmbH of Lich, West Germany. It also carries Computran's 1981 copyright line, address, telephone and telex number. The license is not an inference based on a cabinet's shape or a later collector's description.

Video Games GmbH supplied Super Tank, while Computran advertised its American licensed manufacture and commercial presentation. The division of cabinet construction, board procurement and assembly remains unknown. The Computran name on the American flyer identifies its licensed role rather than original software authorship.

The flyer combines gameplay images, complete-machine photographs and a short account of the action. The second page places the two cabinet forms beside the game description and company contact information. It presents Super Tank as an operator purchase with both a playable system and a physical installation.

What the player does

Super Tank combines movement through a field of hazards with tank combat. The player's tank can move in any direction, including backwards. The first task is to navigate the minefield, clear its mines and avoid or destroy the enemy red tanks. The screens in the Computran flyer show the small battlefield objects distributed around the play area rather than a simulated landscape viewed from inside a tank.

Collecting glowing diamond objects gives a brief period of enhanced armor. During that protection the tank can survive dangers that would ordinarily threaten it. The advertised scoring opportunities come from clearing mines and destroying enemy tanks. The practical challenge is therefore both navigation and firing: the player has to move through the field rather than merely wait in a protected firing position.

Clearing the field leads to an encounter with the larger Super Tank. The flyer identifies its nozzle as the target that must be hit. Destroying that opponent moves the player to another, more demanding screen. This makes the large tank a distinct culmination of the cycle, rather than simply another enemy of the same size and behavior.

The advertising explicitly promotes simultaneous two-player play. Two players can compete against the large tank and against each other, rather than simply alternate turns. Computran emphasized that shared play in its operator-facing promotion, alongside the game's scoring opportunities and the availability of two cabinet forms.

The period flyer describes the minefield, armor objects, red tanks and larger opponent in broad terms. It does not give a complete stage-by-stage rule set or specify every timing, collision and scoring value. Its description records the gameplay Computran advertised to prospective machine buyers.

The machines Computran advertised

The flyer offers upright and cocktail forms. Its dimension table gives the upright as 30 inches deep, 25 inches wide and 68 inches high. The cocktail is listed as 23½ inches deep, 35 inches wide and 31 inches high. These are advertised dimensions for the pictured products; they should not be assumed to describe every later survivor or cabinet fitted with Super Tank hardware.

The upright is presented with dedicated tank artwork and a conventional coin-operated front. The cocktail photograph emphasizes a different installation form, with the game housed in a low table-style cabinet. Offering both forms gave Computran more than one way to place the same licensed game in an arcade or another coin-op location. The flyer does not provide a production breakdown between them.

Its sales language focuses on excitement, player appeal and earnings. That emphasis fits the intended operator audience. The product was being presented as a revenue-producing complete machine, not just as an interesting program or a bare circuit board. The company had to persuade a buyer that its game could attract players and justify a purchase.

The front of the flyer uses a theatrical image: curtains, a covered cabinet and a startled model under the Super Tank title. It is a promotion built around unveiling a product. The reverse does the practical work of showing the machines, explaining the game and supplying contact information. Together the pages preserve the difference between attracting attention and answering an operator's immediate questions.

Service, configuration and production artwork

A Super Tank manual bearing Video Games and Computran branding is reported to cover wiring, edge connections, power requirements, operator settings, coinage, starting-tank and bonus options, and cocktail adaptations. Exact numerical settings remain unconfirmed.

Nevertheless, that recorded scope supports a product with technical documentation and operator support. It reinforces the evidence that Computran handled complete installations. It does not establish that Computran designed the original circuit board, wrote the program or manufactured every power component shown in the documentation. Service literature records the supported system; it is not automatically a design credit.

A surviving Super Tank bezel is reported to bear the name Willis Industries of San Jose and the initials R. Nelson. These markings connect Willis with the product's graphics. The arrangement between the artwork supplier and Computran remains undocumented, and the bezel does not identify the maker of the wooden cabinet.

The artwork connection also links the product to a wider industrial process. Cabinet graphics, screen surrounds and control presentation were manufactured objects with their own suppliers. A licensed foreign game could acquire a distinct American appearance through those parts. Computran's role is visible in that finished presentation even where the original software belongs elsewhere.

Dates, copyright records and distribution

The reported Super Tank copyright entries are PA0000114709 for visual material and TX0000763245 for the computer program. Both are reported to name Computran as claimant and Video Games GmbH in the work-for-hire authorship information, with publication on January 22, 1981 and registration on August 12, 1981. Those entry details await independent confirmation from complete Copyright Office records.

The reported January date concerns Super Tank's publication, followed by registration in August. Computran's legal formation could have preceded either event, but no charter establishes when. Its 1981 flyer supplies independent evidence of company activity during the same year.

Registration VA0000080165 concerns a Russ Berrie textile character, rather than a Computran game. It provides no date for this company or its products. The Super Tank registrations are the separate PA and TX entries identified above.

U.S. Amusements advertised Super Tank in December 1981. That establishes an offer for sale through another New Jersey business. It does not, without more documentation, prove a formal authorized distributorship, a direct purchase from Computran or the precise terms on which the machines were obtained.

Super Tank was promoted as a licensed complete-machine product, with period photographs, operating instructions and advertised dimensions. Computran's name appears alongside the foreign supplier in its commercial and technical presentation. The company's role is considerably clearer here than for the associated games known chiefly through collector cabinets.

Port Man: a licensed game and an uncertain production run

Port Man presents a different problem. Computran's connection is visible, but the scale and route of the product are not. Original-style marquee artwork carries the Computran name and a statement that the product was manufactured under license from Taito Corporation, Japan. That is stronger than a cabinet-family resemblance or a database attribution. It establishes a company-branded Taito licensing presentation.

It does not establish how many machines Computran assembled, the dates on which it offered them, or whether the game entered a normal production run. The preserved story of a single trade-show example comes from a collector's account of a conversation with an unnamed former executive. That account is useful, but it is a different class of evidence from a period production ledger or a signed license.

Port Man is generally dated to 1982 in game catalogs and flyer material. Its wider commercial history includes Taito, Nova and Glak. The Computran marquee identifies a local branded presentation within that wider history, but no complete set of contracts establishes the order in which the companies supplied, licensed or assembled the game.

A dockworker rather than a space pilot

The game's subject is cargo handling. The player moves a dockworker who catches luggage from an overhead conveyor and throws it into a ship's compartments while avoiding hazards. Another phase involves dynamite crates that must be intercepted. It is an unusual occupational theme for a period dominated by more familiar shooting and maze imagery, and helps explain the distinctive artwork.

Different Port Man title screens and supplier presentations have been reported. The broad cargo-handling premise is documented, but the exact software variant in the Computran survivor has not been established through a program comparison. Variant-specific controls, stage rules and scoring remain uncertain.

The cargo-handling premise gave Computran a product distinct from its tank and maze offerings. The coordinated artwork introduced an occupational setting rather than another combat theme. Whether the company intended a continuing catalog entry or a single exhibition presentation remains unresolved.

The dedicated Port Man survivor

The best-known Computran-style survivor has coordinated Port Man presentation in a woodgrain upright. Its marquee and other artwork give it a recognizable product identity. This is more persuasive evidence of a deliberate company presentation than finding the game's board inside an otherwise generic cabinet decades later.

The original-style marquee combines the Computran logo with a Taito licensing line. Both names appear on the same presentation rather than being attributed solely through the cabinet's shape. The marquee connects the American company to a licensed version of the game, while leaving the machine's board supplier and assembly history unresolved.

Artwork on the survivor has also been reported as signed by Willis. That observation fits the broader interest in Computran's production graphics, but the precise supplier arrangement has not been recovered. An artwork signature is not a cabinet-maker's name, a software authorship credit or proof of the terms of a contract.

In January 2015 the survivor's owner reported speaking to a former Computran executive who said the company made one machine for a trade show and chose not to begin production after weak interest. The account gives a possible explanation for the product's apparent scarcity. It does not identify the executive publicly, name the show or supply contemporary documents.

The Nova and Glak complication

A later survivor reported in 2025 is described in a Nova and Glak-style cabinet context while carrying Computran-branded artwork. That combination demonstrates why the product chain cannot be reconstructed from appearance alone. Artwork, cabinet, program and original seller may point toward different participants, especially after decades of repairs and conversions.

A second cabinet carrying Computran artwork complicates the quantity account. The marquee could have circulated separately, been installed by another assembler or moved during a later conversion. The survivor documents a further branded object, but the maker of its complete cabinet has not been established.

Computran-branded Port Man material survives across more than one cabinet context. That wider circulation establishes the existence of additional artwork, without settling how many complete machines Computran assembled. The later example neither verifies nor decisively refutes the former executive's reported one-machine account.

Taito, Nova and Glak appear in Port Man's period or surviving material. Their exact contracts and order of supply remain unknown. The title circulated through multiple commercial presentations, with the Computran licensing line identifying only one part of that wider chain.

One flyer cataloged in a Nova context contains Taito-branded material. Its printed company identity is Taito, despite the archive's broader attribution. Nova remains associated with other Port Man material; the mismatch concerns the identity of that particular flyer.

Port Man's place in the total

Port Man belongs in the strict Computran title count because the company-branded licensing presentation is directly documented. Its inclusion does not mean that normal mass production has been proven. The count deliberately recognizes a documented product relationship while preserving the unresolved commercial status beside it.

Triple Punch: the name inside the glove

Triple Punch's Computran connection was harder to confirm because the important mark is incorporated into the artwork. A surviving original-style marquee includes COMPUTRAN within its yellow glove or fist graphic. That small detail changes the evidence: the company is represented on the game's presentation itself, rather than merely supplied by a modern auction description.

The COMPUTRAN name is incorporated into the original-style marquee itself. Cabinet resemblance and collector descriptions also connect the title with the company, but the readable artwork supplies the direct company identification. The surviving presentation therefore links Triple Punch to Computran even though its license and assembly arrangements remain undocumented.

The game and its other credits

Triple Punch is a maze game built around outlining boxes while avoiding pursuers. Its player character is described as a painter, with limited punches available to disable pursuing enemies temporarily. Its cataloged controls are a four-way joystick and one punch button, with alternating two-player turns and a vertically oriented color raster display. Those are game-level specifications, not measurements of a particular Computran cabinet.

The preservation and software trail credits K.K. International. Period Play Meter coverage in July 1982 identifies Triple Punch as an attraction offered by Thomas Automatics. Those credits do not conflict with the existence of a Computran-branded presentation; they describe other portions of the commercial and software history. What is missing is a document explaining the agreement between the participants.

K.K. International, Thomas Automatics and Computran appear in different parts of Triple Punch's history: the software attribution, period commercial coverage and surviving marquee respectively. No known agreement connects those roles in a complete development, licensing and distribution sequence.

Cabinet evidence and the auction trail

A modern auction catalog described a Triple Punch survivor in Computran and K.K. International terms. The auction's attribution does not establish the original license agreement. Photographs of the survivor also show a SANCO marking on a lower cabinet panel, complicating any attempt to derive the entire product's origin from one descriptive caption.

The company name sits within the illustrated glove rather than in a separate manufacturer panel. It is a small element of the marquee, but its letters identify Computran directly. The lower SANCO marking belongs to a different part of the photographed cabinet and does not replace the marquee's identity.

Known Triple Punch and Bull Frog survivors also support a related woodgrain cabinet pattern. That resemblance is meaningful for the physical-product history. It may reflect a shared shell design, a supplier's cabinet line or common assembly practice. It does not identify the source of the woodwork or prove that every similarly shaped cabinet originally carried a Computran product.

Triple Punch's surviving artwork establishes a Computran-branded presentation. Its production quantity, sales territories and licensing terms remain unknown; no surviving marquee can supply those commercial particulars on its own.

Company-branded presentation

Triple Punch adds a second kind of direct company evidence to the catalog. Super Tank is secured by Computran's own period sales literature. Triple Punch is secured by a surviving company-marked presentation artifact, supported by the separate commercial trail. Port Man supplies a company-marked licensing statement and a different scarcity problem.

Bull Frog: the cabinet with the strongest unfinished trail

Bull Frog is the most important title just outside the strict count. Its association with Computran is substantial, and its dedicated-looking presentation is well documented in collector material. What remains missing is the specific original company marking or period document that closes the attribution to the same standard as the three counted titles.

The game belongs to the Frogger-derivative environment. Its familiar crossing theme is presented under its own frog identity. The surviving artwork includes a Bull Frog marquee, coordinated screen surround and side graphics, with the phrase “Jump for Your Life” appearing in the documented presentation. The woodgrain cabinet and compact controls give it a distinct physical form.

Those features are evidence of a deliberate commercial presentation. They do not independently identify the programmer, circuit-board manufacturer or finished-machine supplier. An appealing and internally consistent cabinet can still lack the manufacturer plate or sales paperwork needed to assign the complete machine securely to a particular company.

A surviving Bull Frog cabinet

The cabinet photographed by DonPanetta in 2025 preserves Bull Frog's dedicated-looking exterior. Its woodgrain enclosure and coordinated game graphics connect it with the cabinet family discussed here. The image documents the survivor's appearance, while its original seller and the source of its board remain unresolved.

Other documented exterior and interior views suggest a common cabinet family and a paired-board assembly. Some images show enough to compare layout, but not enough to read every legend or ROM label. Board count and position can support a hardware hypothesis; they cannot establish authorship where the identifying markings are unreadable.

One photographed survivor has a green control-panel treatment, a red ball-top joystick and white buttons. These features describe that machine as it survives. No original sales specification establishes whether the colors and components were standard across Bull Frog production.

Other Bull Frog machines in the legal record

The 1984 opinion in United States v. Steerwell Leisure Corp., 598 F. Supp. 171, records Bull Frog games at four Buffalo-area locations associated with Steerwell placements or leases. Midway expert Robert Landry examined one at a Buffalo restaurant and reported that its electronic board lacked copyright information. An FBI agent also described its play to an attorney for Sega, who considered it equivalent to Frogger.

The court was considering probable cause and pretrial motions, not establishing the manufacturer of every game bearing that title. Computran is not named in the opinion. The Buffalo machines therefore document Bull Frog's circulation and its appearance in infringement allegations without identifying them as Computran products. Their original cabinet construction, board markings and supplier arrangements remain unknown.

FROG and Video Games GmbH

A six-page manual described as “Frog (Bull Frog)” has a reported cabinet provenance. In 2005 its owner stated that he had found it inside a Bull Frog machine and scanned it. The cover uses FROG and a capsule VIDEO GAMES logo; the technical sections describe FROG A and B boards.

The logo is visually consistent with branding on the known Video Games GmbH Super Tank documentation. The two-board description also offers a meaningful comparison with the paired assembly seen in Bull Frog interior photographs. Taken together, these are better clues than a title-only database entry because they concern a physical manual, its reported location and a specific hardware arrangement.

The missing step is direct identification. A manual found in a cabinet may have belonged to the installed game, but cabinets can retain paperwork through conversions. A matching logo can connect a document to a supplier, but it does not establish a license to Computran. A paired board can fit a manual's description without yet exposing a readable manufacturer name.

The manual's logo and paired-board description suggest a possible Video Games GmbH connection for Bull Frog/FROG hardware or documentation. A direct commercial chain linking Bull Frog, Computran and Video Games GmbH remains unverified. Neither the manual's reported cabinet location nor the visual logo match establishes a Computran license.

The Museum's catalog also labels the maker of the manual-associated Frog game as unknown. That is consistent with leaving the company bridge open. Its label does not defeat the physical clues, but it does not supply the absent original attribution either. The title and the document require further evidence to be matched securely.

Labels, boards and conversions

Surviving Bull Frog uprights may retain identifying evidence inside their cabinets. Readable manufacturer labels, board legends, ROM markings and manuals could establish suppliers or earlier installations. The wiring arrangement could also be compared with the FROG A/B system described in the six-page manual.

The most valuable future photographs would show the complete machine and its interior before any changes, then close views of identifying details. A label photographed only in close-up can lose its location; an interior photographed only from a distance can lose its text. Both context and legibility are needed to establish what belongs to what.

Unused mounting holes, overlaid control graphics, mismatched connectors and earlier artwork can survive a cabinet conversion. They preserve the machine's operating history alongside its current title. Bull Frog's cabinet and installed board may therefore have separate origins, even where the exterior graphics appear coordinated.

Bull Frog remains outside the three verified title relationships. Its coordinated artwork and surviving cabinets demonstrate a commercial presentation, but its specific Computran attribution lacks the company marking or period paperwork available for the counted titles.

Amigo: a repeated attribution without the original company bridge

Amigo is the other significant associated title outside the strict total. Modern game references repeatedly connect it to Computran, and collector accounts associate it with the company's cabinets and hardware. Repetition alone, however, does not identify the original source of an attribution. A modern list may be repeating another list rather than preserving a company advertisement or readable manufacturer label.

The game's broad relationship is to Amidar, the line-tracing maze game. Contemporary litigation discussed Amigo as an alleged Amidar infringement. That supplies period evidence about the name and the way the program was understood by investigators. It does not automatically identify Computran as the supplier of every Amigo board or machine mentioned in those proceedings.

In the Steerwell opinion, FBI Agent Campanella described Amigo to Special Agent Warren Flagg, who had investigated infringements of Konami's Amidar copyright. Flagg considered Amigo an infringement. The opinion records that comparison as part of the probable-cause evidence; it does not identify Computran as the programmer or supplier. The legal account establishes a period use of the Amigo name independently of later company lists.

Possible hardware relationships have been noted among Amigo, Frogger-derived equipment and other maze-game boards. Board layouts, component legends, ROM labels and manuals would be needed to establish the connections. No confirmed board-family identification currently ties the associated Amigo machines to Computran.

Some preservation descriptions identify a Frogger bootleg as running on “Amigo?” hardware, explicitly leaving the board attribution uncertain. A shared hardware arrangement, if confirmed, would connect the programs technically; it would still leave the supplier of a complete American machine to be identified separately.

Known survivor photographs show no unmistakable original Computran marking for Amigo. A company advertisement, original manual, invoice, board label or manufacturer plate could change its status. Until then, Amigo deserves a place in the discussion of the company's associated catalog, accompanied by the reason it is not counted as verified.

Cabinets, artwork and the risk of counting conversions

The woodgrain cabinet family is a major part of Computran's surviving identity. Bull Frog and Triple Punch examples suggest a shared or closely related upright pattern: a narrow body, recessed display, projecting control area, coin door and title-specific graphics. Such similarities can reveal production choices even when the wooden shell's supplier is unknown.

Cabinet evidence is strongest when several independent features agree. External profile alone is a weaker comparison than profile combined with dimensions, joinery, internal blocking, control-panel construction and original mounting points. Two machines can look alike because they came from the same cabinet maker, because one copied the other, or because both were converted from a common earlier product.

Artwork introduces a further layer. A marquee can be swapped without replacing a cabinet; a control panel can be drilled for another game; side graphics can be applied over previous artwork. That flexibility made arcade equipment useful to operators. It also makes a surviving cabinet a record of its entire working life rather than necessarily an untouched record of its original sale.

Collector albums associated with Computran include other titles, among them Naughty Boy and Pac-Man cabinet examples. These remain cabinet or conversion leads. They are not additions to the company's verified product count. The presence of a famous game's board inside a familiar shell does not prove Computran originally manufactured, licensed or marketed that installation.

For the same reason, a census of surviving cabinets cannot be turned into a production total. Private collections are incomplete, their entries change, and some machines retain only part of their original identity. A company that assembled few units might have widely circulated artwork; a company that sold more machines might leave almost no intact survivors. The present record does not allow those quantities to be estimated reliably.

A surviving machine combines a program, a circuit board, an enclosure and a commercial identity. Computran's marked artwork identifies part of that assembly; the Super Tank license identifies another relationship. For several associated cabinets, the original board supplier and final assembler remain unknown.

Nintendo's 1982 enforcement campaign

The September 18, 1982 Cash Box report places Computran within Nintendo's copyright-enforcement campaign. It reports Nintendo's account of preliminary injunctions in New Jersey involving roughly 100 defendants and seizures on July 27–29. Computran, Inc. is among the named targets. The article provides a consequential contemporary connection, but not a complete Computran case history.

Cash Box names Crazy Kong and Gongorilla in its separate St. Louis paragraph. Its New Jersey account does not name Computran's particular machines or give an August 13 injunction date. The named titles and seizure details from the St. Louis action cannot be assigned to the New Jersey company.

Cash Box reported damages claims as pending. Its account records Nintendo's enforcement assertions and injunction targets, rather than a final judgment on Computran's conduct. The company's seized inventory, suppliers, sales volume and final liability remain unidentified.

Cash Box uses the shortened name “Computran, Inc.” in the New Jersey account. The period company context connects the report with the Point Pleasant Beach venture, but the original defendant schedule has not been recovered to confirm its complete legal name.

Crazy Kong, Gongorilla and Congorilla

The broader Crazy Kong history explains why genuine-looking boards and license labels can coexist with unauthorized commercial channels. The legal record describes Falcon's license restrictions as limited to Japan. A board associated with that arrangement does not automatically carry permission for an American distributor to sell it in the United States.

The published Nintendo v. Elcon litigation supplies a separate supply-chain example: Elcon obtained Falcon-appearing Crazy Kong boards from Artic International in New Jersey. That is contextual evidence about the market. No direct Artic-to-Computran transaction has been established, and the Michigan proceeding is not a substitute for Computran's unidentified New Jersey case.

The supplier named in Nintendo v. Elcon is Artic International of New Jersey. It appears in that proceeding as the source of Falcon-appearing boards supplied to Elcon. The separate finished-cabinet brands and the limited territory of Falcon's permission complicate the American distribution history.

Game Machine's December 1, 1982 account places the Japanese licensing dispute within a specific sequence. Nintendo granted Kyoei Co. manufacturing rights in September 1981, limiting quantities and prohibiting exports. Kyoei then transferred those rights to Falcon. Nintendo alleged that Falcon produced beyond the permitted quantity and exported games without authorization. That dispute concerned the scope of a Japanese arrangement rather than a general license for American cabinet sales.

On October 13, 1982 Nintendo and Nintendo Leisure System brought a full civil action in Kyoto District Court seeking to halt unauthorized manufacture, sales and exports and to recover about 190 million yen. Game Machine described the action as following earlier injunctive relief. Its report attributed to Nintendo an allegation that unauthorized production exceeded 9,000 units, alongside exports barred by the license. Those were allegations in the Japanese dispute, not quantities assigned to Computran. No direct Falcon-to-Computran sale is established by that account, and the Japanese proceeding remains separate from Nintendo's New Jersey action.

Collector evidence connects Crazy Kong and Congorilla-family equipment with Computran-style cabinets. That association is worth preserving, but the term “style” must retain its meaning. It describes observed cabinet features or collector attribution, not an original invoice or an adjudicated distribution chain.

A photographed Congorilla rear label refers to manufacture under Nintendo license and carries the identifier TKG: 04049. Its wording and position are valuable provenance clues. They do not establish who applied the label, whether the finished American cabinet was covered by permission, or whether Computran assembled that particular machine. A license claim printed on an object must be evaluated against the scope of the permission it invokes.

Accordingly, Crazy Kong, Gongorilla and Congorilla remain associated context outside the strict Computran total. The company's name in an enforcement report is not a title-specific manufacturing credit. The original New Jersey complaint, defendant schedule, orders and any machine inventories could materially change this part of the history.

A separate FBI trail

Contemporary reporting also describes separate FBI counterfeit-game investigations involving U.S. Amusements and Bagatelle Amusements. Several business names overlap with Nintendo's civil enforcement reporting, but the records do not establish that these accounts describe one raid or one legal action.

A November 1, 1982 syndicated report described an FBI affidavit concerning a July undercover purchase from U.S. Amusements. Thirty alleged counterfeit machines were reported in that purchase, including two called “Bull Frogs.” Attorney Barry Slotnik disputed the affidavit's characterization. The account does not identify the two machines' manufacturer, board supplier, cabinet maker or program version, and the shared title does not connect them to Computran.

Billboard's August 21, 1982 coverage separately reported a July 27 FBI raid involving U.S. Amusements and Bagatelle Amusements. That reporting places the criminal investigation alongside the civil enforcement activity, without establishing that Computran was raided or that all the proceedings concerned the same machines.

U.S. Amusements had also advertised Super Tank, which demonstrates that the business offered a known Computran-manufactured licensed product. That fact does not identify the supplier of its other games or prove that all its product lines came through Computran. Commercial proximity is an investigative lead, not a complete transaction history.

Still visible in 1983

Computran did not vanish from the trade record immediately after the 1982 enforcement report. A Play Meter list published in February 1983 names Computran Industries, Inc. among Amusement Operators Expo exhibitors, with the list stated to be current as of January 1. This documents planned or listed participation rather than confirmed physical attendance.

Computran's actual attendance remains unconfirmed. No known booth photograph, floor-plan allocation, named representative, displayed product or post-show account establishes its presence, and no withdrawal notice has been identified. The exhibitor list records a plan current as of January 1.

More concrete commercial visibility appears in the March 1, 1983 directory. Eastern Coin Machine Ltd. of Baltimore lists Computran among the manufacturers it represented. This establishes a distributor connection in the published record. It does not identify the Computran titles Eastern Coin stocked, whether it was selling existing inventory, or whether fresh machines were still being manufactured.

A later Eastern Coin manufacturer list in Cash Box, July 2, 1983, omits Computran. Because the later item actually lists represented lines, the omission is meaningful negative evidence. It is not a legal dissolution record and cannot supply an exact closure date. A change in representation can occur while a corporation remains in existence.

No dependable later Computran commercial listing is known. That makes March 1983 the present last-positive boundary, not the proven final day of business. Later legal inactivity, cessation of production and formal dissolution are distinct events that may have occurred at different times.

SMS Manufacturing: continuity with an overlap

SMS Manufacturing is essential to explaining what followed Computran, but it is not presently documented as a simple new name for the same legal company. The New Jersey State Commission of Investigation's September 1991 report records Salvatore Mirando's account of leaving U.S. Amusements and forming SMS in 1982 with Vincent Storino and Pasquale Storino, each contributing $10,000. The statement is attributed testimony preserved in a government report.

Period material places SMS at the same Broadway address, post-office box and telephone number used by Computran. The Storino participation supplies personnel continuity. SMS advertisements in 1987 and 1990 use telex 132168, supplying another connection with the earlier Super Tank advertising, extending the trail beyond a shared local address.

Those are strong indicators of operational continuity. They show that the two businesses belonged to a connected commercial environment and shared infrastructure. They do not, by themselves, identify an asset-sale agreement, a merger, a parent-subsidiary structure or a formal legal rename.

The chronology rules out a neat overnight replacement. SMS was operating in 1982 while Computran remained visible in early-1983 directories and exhibitor material. The two names overlapped. Whatever practical transfer, division or change of emphasis may have occurred, it was not simply a sequence in which one documented name ended before the other began.

Court and trade records describe SMS offering Hi-Lo Double Up Joker Poker by September or October 1982. A later Play Meter profile describes its start in Point Pleasant in 1982. These sources support an independently described beginning for SMS, rather than supplying a statement that Computran merely changed its sign.

The commercial overlap also appears in the November 20, 1982 Cash Box AMOA Expo directory. S.M.S. Manufacturing Corporation is listed at 3 Broadway, P.O. Box 1872, Point Pleasant Beach, with telephone 201-295-2777. The shared street address, box and telephone connect its public contact details to Computran during the period when both names were appearing in trade material.

The later appellate opinion in M. Kramer Manufacturing Co. v. Andrews, 783 F.2d 421, identifies Pasquale Storino as an SMS principal and describes SMS selling Hi-Lo Double Up Joker Poker by September or October 1982. It also records a New Jersey copyright action filed by Foy and Storino through SMS on February 16, 1983, Civil Action No. 83-554. The suit concerned video card-game rights and belongs to SMS's history; it is not the missing Nintendo action against Computran.

One footnote in that opinion renders the company name as “SMNS,” while the narrative uses SMS. The court record establishes commercial and legal activity by the connected business during Computran's last documented trade period. It does not identify a merger, asset transfer or corporate name change between the two.

A business that continued beyond the Computran name

Later SMS material places it at 1000 Airport Road in Lakewood by December 1985 and at 601 Prospect Street by 1988. Its continued telex identifier is useful because the link survives a geographic move. The phone and street address connected the overlapping local ventures; the telex extends the infrastructure connection into the later business.

SMS's later product range included merchandisers, redemption equipment and conventional coin-operated products as well as card games. Its manufacturing activity extended beyond video poker, while its later products belonged to the SMS business rather than Computran's catalog.

Later documents use both SMS Manufacturing Corporation and SMS Manufacturing Company, Inc. Federal material records a doing-business-as relationship by 1990. The exact original New Jersey charter identity remains unresolved, so the variations in the printed name do not establish a separate formation sequence.

An appellate account describes the January 1990 transaction as a division of the businesses: Mirando retained the gambling-machine operation while the Storinos acquired the Point Pleasant boardwalk properties. That arrangement is more specific than a simple purchase of the Storinos' entire interest by Mirando.

These later facts help show that the Storino manufacturing environment persisted after Computran ceased appearing in the accessible trade trail. They do not make every later SMS product part of Computran's catalog. Nor do they settle Computran's corporate end. Operational continuity and legal succession remain separate findings.

An unrelated Computran

Computran Systems Corporation of Hackensack, New Jersey belongs to a different history. It was associated with traffic-control engineering, not this Point Pleasant Beach arcade venture. Its records can appear in the same searches, and its New Jersey location can make the confusion seem plausible.

The Hackensack company's patents, lawsuits, officers and corporate dates belong to its traffic-control business. Its address, legal identity and activity differ from those of Computran Industries in Point Pleasant Beach. No corporate connection between the two companies has been established.

What remains open

The unresolved questions are specific. Computran's formation and dissolution dates still require corporate records. Its exact relationship to SMS still requires an original legal or business document. The 1982 Nintendo matter still requires the original New Jersey case identification and company-specific papers.

For the games, the most important missing bridge is Bull Frog/FROG's direct company and supplier identification. Amigo needs comparable primary attribution. Port Man needs production and licensing-chain documentation. Triple Punch needs the agreement or sales material that explains the already visible company-branded presentation.

Unresolved physical details include original manufacturer labels, the installation history of associated boards, and the actual functions of the Broadway and Vincentown addresses. Reggie Alexander's specific contribution to Computran's games or manufacturing operation also remains unknown.

Computran's surviving record is small but distinctive. Its own Super Tank advertising establishes declared international licensing. Its marked Port Man and Triple Punch artwork establishes further product presentations. Its associated frog and maze games retain useful unfinished provenance trails. Its trade listings continue into 1983, while SMS appears alongside it and later carries connected personnel and infrastructure forward.

Documentary notes for this history

Publication and directory dates

Computran's earliest documented arcade activity is in 1981, independently supported by its Super Tank flyer. The reported January 22 publication date belongs to the game; the August 12 registration date belongs to its copyright entries. Neither date establishes the formation of the New Jersey corporation. The March 1983 distributor listing likewise supplies a commercial reference rather than a dissolution date.

The March 1982 Play Meter directory has an erroneous 1981 footer on its Computran page. The issue cover and volume identification establish the correct year. Its officer names and engineering title are evidence for 1982, while the separate Super Tank flyer establishes earlier company activity.

Documented product roles

The three verified title relationships cover different roles. Super Tank has period licensed-manufacture advertising; Port Man has company-marked Taito licensing artwork; Triple Punch has a direct Computran name in its marquee. Original software development and equivalent production runs have not been established across the three.

Bull Frog and Amigo lack the direct company identification available for the three counted titles. Crazy Kong and Congorilla-family equipment appears in cabinet and enforcement material, but no title-specific Computran manufacturing relationship is established. Those associations remain separate from the verified total.

Testimony and cabinet provenance

The Port Man one-machine account is preserved as a collector's report of an unnamed former executive's statement. The SMS founding account is attributed to Mirando's testimony summarized in the New Jersey report. These accounts are testimony rather than an original charter or production ledger. Physical logos and license lines are more direct for the existence of a particular company-branded presentation, but remain limited in what they say about quantities and contracts.

The Bull Frog manual's reported cabinet location accompanies its FROG title, capsule VIDEO GAMES logo and A/B board descriptions. Its original installation history is unknown: the paperwork may have remained in the enclosure through later equipment changes. The manual and photographed boards suggest a connection without establishing an uninterrupted original assembly.

Period publications and surviving objects

The principal product sources are the 1981 Computran Super Tank flyer; reported Super Tank technical and copyright records; the March 1982 Play Meter directory; company-marked Port Man and Triple Punch artwork; period coverage of both titles; and early-1983 exhibitor and Eastern Coin listings. Collector accounts supply survivor descriptions and attributed oral testimony.

Period SMS address and communications material, the 1991 New Jersey State Commission of Investigation report, court records and later trade coverage document the connected business. The September 18, 1982 Cash Box report names Computran in Nintendo's New Jersey campaign. Separate legal proceedings document the wider market without establishing this company's final case outcome.
