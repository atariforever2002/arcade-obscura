Mexico’s brief bridge into the Neo Geo world

EVOGA Entertainment left behind only two released arcade games. On paper, that should make it a small story.

It is not.

EVOGA grew out of a Mexican circle of arcade players, anime fans and SNK enthusiasts who became testers and event collaborators around Plus Electronics, SNK's Mexican business network and the late-1990s arcade trade. Within a remarkably short period, members of that circle were sitting in meetings in Osaka, designing characters and game systems for Japanese arcade hardware, working alongside BrezzaSoft and Noise Factory, and receiving visible company credit on commercial machines sold internationally.

The result was Evolution Soccer in 2001 and Rage of the Dragons in 2002. Neither was the work of a conventional self-contained studio. Both were products of a cross-border development pipeline in which creative direction, planning, graphics, programming, sound, hardware expertise, investment and publishing were divided among companies on opposite sides of the Pacific.

That production structure is what makes EVOGA historically important. Its story shows how arcade games could be created outside the familiar Japanese and American centers at the end of the Neo Geo era — and how fragile that opportunity could be.

The company also left behind a larger shadow catalog: an unfinished Rage of the Dragons 2, a three-on-three basketball concept, a platform game called Geometrics, other early concepts, a proposed console conversion, and, years later, a documented attempt to revive Evolution Soccer as Evolution Soccer Club Edition. Some of those projects existed only on paper. Others appear to have reached more advanced planning. None changed EVOGA's released arcade total.

For Arcade Side B, that distinction matters. EVOGA's history is not a hidden ten-game catalog. It is the story of how a Mexican creative team made its way into Japanese arcade production, released two games, built a much larger set of ambitions around them, and then disappeared almost as quickly as it had arrived.

Before EVOGA: a fan circle becomes useful to the arcade business

Mario Vargas, credited in EVOGA's games as Mr.Vo, later described the company's beginnings as a group of seven friends connected by anime, videogames and the Mexican arcade scene. The core group was Sergio ("Serg"), Ángel ("Angel Sword"), Erick ("Darb"), Eduardo ("Edu"), Mario ("Mr.Vo"), José Luis ("Bel-Mont") and Valen or Valente.

This group did not begin as a formal game studio. It grew into the business through the existing arcade and distribution network around Plus Electronics. Vargas recalled the group helping with SNK-related events, testing and market feedback. When Plus began exploring a project with the Japanese company BrezzaSoft, the seven friends were asked to develop ideas for a game aimed at the local market. Because there were seven of them, they used the name HEPTA while working on the project in their spare time.

That precursor matters because later summaries often flatten the story into a neat founding date: EVOGA was created in 2000, then it made two games. Vargas's account is messier and more useful. The organization evolved in stages.

The first stage was the fan/tester circle. The second was HEPTA, a seven-person part-time project group. The third came after the project became serious enough to require regular office work and travel to Japan.

Ángel Torres later explained the company name itself: EVOGA meant “Evolution Games.” He said the longer name was rejected and that the group briefly considered EVOGAM before settling on EVOGA. That explanation comes from EVOGA's own director rather than a later reconstruction.

In September 2000, Vargas and Ángel Torres traveled to BrezzaSoft's headquarters in Osaka for the project that would become Evolution Soccer. Vargas says that when they returned to Mexico, Plus decided the group needed to be more deeply involved and hired the remaining members full-time. Several of the original seven had already begun leaving for personal reasons. Vargas describes the surviving core at that point as Ángel, Eduardo and himself — and says that EVOGA had just been born.

That does not mean the other members became irrelevant. Several names from the original circle recur in credits and later EVOGA activity. It does mean that the neat phrase "seven founders of EVOGA" hides an important transition. For publication purposes, it is more accurate to describe the seven as the HEPTA precursor/original project circle, with the full-time EVOGA operating team crystallizing after the 2000 Osaka trip.

Plus Electronics: incubator, employer and business bridge

EVOGA's relationship with Plus Electronics is one of the areas where later accounts have become oversimplified.

The evidence does not support treating EVOGA as nothing more than a Plus trade name. It also does not yet prove that EVOGA was a wholly owned Plus subsidiary.

What can be supported is a two-layer relationship.

Operationally, Plus was essential. Vargas says Plus called the HEPTA group into the BrezzaSoft project, provided office space, hired the remaining team full-time after the September 2000 Osaka visit, financed or invested in the Japanese collaborations, and handled high-level business negotiations. Plus was the bridge that turned a group of enthusiasts and testers into a professional development operation.

Legally, however, EVOGA acquired its own identity. A Mexican federal trademark notice published in 2007 identifies EVOGA ENTERTAIMENT, S.A. de C.V. as the registered owner of the E S Evolution Soccer y Diseño trademark. The spelling "ENTERTAIMENT" is the spelling in the official record and should be preserved when referring specifically to the legal entity.

That government record rules out one common oversimplification: EVOGA was not merely an informal label used inside Plus. EVOGA held intellectual property in its own corporate name.

The exact ownership structure remains unresolved. No incorporation deed or shareholder record has yet been recovered showing whether Plus owned EVOGA outright, held a partial stake, or simply financed and employed the group while a separate company was established.

The safest summary is therefore also the most informative: EVOGA grew out of Plus Electronics' SNK distribution and testing network; Plus recruited, employed and financed the development group and served as the business bridge to Japan, while EVOGA subsequently operated under its own Mexican corporate identity.

Evolution Soccer: the first Mexican-Japanese production bridge

The first released EVOGA game was Evolution Soccer, released for BrezzaSoft's Crystal System hardware in 2001. Preservation databases date the arcade release to August 31, 2001; the surviving software identifies itself as © 2001 Evoga Entertainment. The game is preserved today in MAME as a four-player-capable Crystal/Zealer-board title with an eight-way joystick and four action buttons.

The hardware context is worth remembering. BrezzaSoft was trying to establish the Crystal System as a new 2D arcade platform at exactly the moment the old SNK/Neo Geo business was fragmenting. Evolution Soccer therefore was not just EVOGA's first game; it was tied to an attempted post-Neo-Geo arcade platform whose commercial life proved very short.

Database descriptions have often made the development attribution look contradictory. Some list EVOGA as developer. Others emphasize BrezzaSoft. The game's own credits and the surviving interviews show why both names appear: Evolution Soccer was a co-production whose responsibilities crossed company lines.

The game credits identify EVOGA as producer and carry a 2001 EVOGA Entertainment copyright. The staff list includes Radel as director, Edu as planner, programmers YuritaRo and Hara, a mixed graphics team, research credits including Zhila and CasDom V, and voice credits including Zhila and Furank.

The strongest current interpretation is that EVOGA led the creative, planning and production side, while BrezzaSoft supplied the Crystal System environment and substantial programming/technical support.

That interpretation is reinforced by Vargas's account. He says Plus originally asked the Mexican team to develop concepts for a local-market game with BrezzaSoft. BrezzaSoft's programmers favored a football game because of budget, schedule and existing experience. The September 2000 Osaka trip then pulled the Mexican group much further into the project than simply delivering local ideas.

The finished game also retained a distinctly Mexican component. The software supports a Mexico region alongside the overseas configuration, and the Mexican setting includes local club-style teams in addition to the international tournament structure. The use of altered names and logos in some local content reflects the familiar licensing compromises of arcade sports games rather than a different product lineage.

The important point for EVOGA's history is not that the company wrote every line of code. It did not. The important point is that the Mexican team had crossed the line from market consultation into credited game production.

How Evolution Soccer played

Evolution Soccer was built as a four-player arcade football game rather than a home-console simulation transplanted unchanged into a cabinet. The preserved machine definition gives each player an eight-way joystick and four buttons. Those buttons cover low passing/shooting/sliding, high passing/tackling, turbo, and special techniques. The software runs at 320×240 and is preserved under the MAME machine ID evosocc; current preservation records still flag imperfect sound emulation, which is useful to note when comparing emulated play with original hardware.

The game also contains an unusually visible regional split. In its Mexico configuration it includes Mexican club-style teams alongside the international sides, while the overseas configuration focuses on national teams. That is a small technical detail, but historically it fits the way the project began: Plus and the future EVOGA group were not simply asked to imitate an existing Japanese sports title. They were being brought in precisely because they could supply local-market ideas and references that the Japanese partner did not have on its own.

The game's staff list tells the same story from another angle. EVOGA-linked handles appear in direction, planning, research and voice work, while the programming and portions of the art pipeline are shared with Japanese personnel. The credits therefore look mixed because the production actually was mixed. Treating the game as exclusively Mexican or exclusively Japanese erases the most interesting thing about it.

Why the Crystal System matters

The Crystal System was a very different starting point from the Neo Geo hardware EVOGA would use next. The preserved Evolution Soccer board family uses a SE3208 main CPU and MagicEyes VRender0 technology rather than SNK's familiar MVS architecture. For EVOGA, that meant the first commercial project was inseparable from BrezzaSoft's technical environment and from an arcade platform that never became an enduring standard.

That short platform life is one reason Evolution Soccer is easily overlooked today. The game did not acquire the decades of hardware familiarity, ports, cartridge collecting and tournament memory that helped preserve the visibility of many Neo Geo titles. Yet it is the game that proves EVOGA had already become more than a localization or testing group before Rage of the Dragons existed.

That distinction becomes even clearer with EVOGA's next project.

Project DD: from Double Dragon idea to Rage of the Dragons

Development of what became Rage of the Dragons began while Evolution Soccer was still in progress.

Vargas recalled arriving in Osaka in early 2001 with a very small preliminary design and character concept for a fighting game. The project was developed with Noise Factory, a Japanese studio with experience on Neo Geo hardware. The original concept was connected to the 1995 Neo Geo Double Dragon fighting game.

A contemporary EVOGA interview from August 2002 is especially valuable because it was published while the game was current, not reconstructed years later. EVOGA said the project had begun in November 2000 with the first meeting with the Japanese team and had taken roughly a year to develop. The interview identifies an EVOGA-side team and separately describes a Noise Factory team of programmers, testers and dot/pixel staff, with additional sound and voice contributors.

Most importantly, EVOGA itself explained the practical division of labor. Noise Factory handled the Neo Geo programming, pixel-level character production, sound work, and gameplay/planning tuning. EVOGA supplied much of the game system, character concepts, move and power concepts, story, character profiles, stage design, graphics and other creative planning.

The ROM-derived credits line up unusually well with that account.

Ángel Torres appears as Angel Sword among the directors. Mario Vargas appears as Mr.Vo as sub-director, planner and artist. Edu is credited in planning and character design. Bel Mont, Darb, Hikaru Suf, Shin Ryu and other EVOGA-associated handles appear across character art, background work, testing and special thanks. On the programming side, Hidenari Mamoto, Kazuaki Ezato and Hiroshi Hishikawa appear in roles consistent with Noise Factory's technical contribution. The game closes with the credit Presented by Evoga Entertainment.

A period Game Watch item also linked Noise Factory's site directly to a March 2002 Rage of the Dragons location test at Neo Geo Land Esaka, independently confirming Noise Factory's public development role.

The result is one of the clearest examples in EVOGA's history of why a simple "developer" field is inadequate.

The arcade/MVS release is generally dated June 6, 2002, while the Neo Geo AES home cartridge followed on September 20, 2002. This distinction also helps explain why some later databases collapse the arcade and home dates into a single September release.

Rage of the Dragons was not merely designed in Mexico and coded anonymously in Japan, nor was EVOGA a name attached to a Noise Factory game. It was a genuine cross-border co-development whose responsibilities can be reconstructed in unusual detail.

How Rage of the Dragons played

The finished game is a two-player, four-button Neo Geo fighter built around teams of two. Fourteen fighters are selectable in normal play, with Abubo and Johann filling boss roles. The standard Neo Geo layout maps weak and strong punches and kicks across the familiar A–D buttons. Preservation records identify the MVS game as NGM-264.

The tag structure was more than a presentation gimmick. Players could switch partners, recover some health while a character rested, and perform team-based attacks. The design also used a universal launcher/combo mechanic commonly called First Impact, giving every character a route into a preset follow-up sequence. Stage-edge objects and other environmental touches helped the fights feel less like a straight copy of SNK's own one-on-one formula.

That design choice makes sense in light of EVOGA's documented responsibilities. The Mexican side was heavily involved in character concepts, move ideas, system planning, story and stages; Noise Factory translated and tuned those ideas inside a mature Neo Geo production pipeline. The result feels much closer to a deliberate game-design collaboration than Evolution Soccer, where EVOGA had entered an already-defined sports/hardware project.

The roster itself also carries the project's international character. Fighters include characters associated with Mexico and Latin America alongside Japanese, American and other archetypes, while names such as Billy, Jimmy and Abubo preserve traces of the abandoned Double Dragon lineage. EVOGA's team was not hiding its influences; it was reworking them into a property it could actually ship.

From experimental hardware to a mature arcade platform

The hardware change between EVOGA's two released games is revealing. Evolution Soccer depended on BrezzaSoft's short-lived Crystal System. Rage of the Dragons moved onto the enormously well-understood Neo Geo MVS platform. That did not make the second project simple, but it let the production divide responsibilities more cleanly: Noise Factory already knew how to make the Neo Geo perform, while EVOGA could push deeper into design, character art, planning and presentation.

That is also why the phrase “developed by EVOGA” can be simultaneously understandable and incomplete. EVOGA was central to what Rage of the Dragons was; Noise Factory was central to making that design function as a finished Neo Geo product. Playmore then occupied the publishing/rightsholder position on the commercial release. The surviving evidence supports all three layers.

The Double Dragon connection — what the evidence actually says

The relationship to Double Dragon is often repeated in simplified form: EVOGA wanted to make an official sequel, failed to obtain the rights, and converted the project into an homage.

The first part is solid. EVOGA's own 2002 interview says the project was developed as a sequel to the 1995 Neo Geo Double Dragon fighting game before the concept changed. The finished game preserved obvious echoes of that lineage in names, character ideas and visual references.

The more specific story that EVOGA formally sought the rights and was refused is much harder to document directly. Later sources repeat it frequently, but the strongest period EVOGA material does not clearly establish the exact legal negotiation.

For Arcade Side B, the distinction is worth preserving. The project began as a Double Dragon sequel concept; the finished game was not an official Double Dragon product; the exact rights-negotiation story should be attributed cautiously rather than presented as an independently proven fact.

EVOGA also said in 2002 that no former Technōs Japan personnel had participated in the development.

EVOGA and The King of Fighters: influence without ownership

EVOGA's relationship with SNK and the post-SNK production network produced another historical trap: games that include EVOGA ideas or staff influence are sometimes folded into the company's output.

That should not be done.

Ángel Torres later described EVOGA's contribution to The King of Fighters 2001 as ideas, testing and one character. Later retrospective accounts connect the team to Mexican cultural references and to the inclusion of the character Ángel.

For The King of Fighters 2002, Torres described EVOGA's involvement more modestly: ideas and testing. EVOGA also supported Mexican presentations and distribution activity around those games.

Those are meaningful historical contributions, especially for understanding the Mexico-Japan relationship, but neither title becomes an EVOGA-developed arcade game.

The same rule applies in the opposite direction to Power Instinct Matrimelee. Several Rage of the Dragons characters later appeared there because Noise Factory reused them. Torres said EVOGA was not consulted in advance and chose not to make a dispute of the matter.

That makes Matrimelee part of the EVOGA story, but not part of the EVOGA catalog.

The controlled released count remains two.

Mexican ideas inside somebody else's series

The KOF connection becomes more informative when its individual contributions are described without changing their category. Torres characterized the 2001 work as ideas, testing and one character. A later Atomix retrospective, checked with Torres, Vargas and José Luis Corona Belmont, supplies additional cultural context: material relating to Mexican lucha libre and a book that helped inspire the use of Mexico City's Franz Mayer Museum. These are examples of local knowledge entering an established Japanese fighting-game series through the Mexican collaboration.

Torres's account of the character Ángel also retains the atmosphere of the relationship. He linked the inclusion to competitive KOF sessions and a wager made around the time Rage was being negotiated. That is a participant's retrospective recollection and should be read as such. He expressly said the character did not look like him. The released game's Japanese-side art credits provide another reason to distinguish the request or influence behind a character's inclusion from the work that produced its final appearance and animation.

For KOF 2002, his description is narrower: ideas and testing. He also recalled supporting the Mexican presentations and distribution of both games. Those commercial and promotional activities are relevant to EVOGA's relationship with the audience that had helped bring the group into the business. They remain distinct from responsibility for programming, the complete art pipeline or formal development of the KOF products.

Matrimelee carries the connection forward through four characters: Jimmy, Lynn, Elias and Mr. Jones. Torres said Noise Factory used them without first consulting EVOGA, and that EVOGA chose not to make a dispute of it. Vargas remembered learning of the appearances after the game had been released, following the loss of contact with Noise Factory. His uncertainty about the selection should remain intact. Their recollections describe character reuse and the relationship's aftermath, not a documented new co-development agreement.

This distinction gives EVOGA a presence in a larger arcade history without artificially enlarging its own output. Ideas, testing, distribution support, character inclusion and later character reuse each tell a different part of the story. None requires the claim that EVOGA developed KOF 2001, KOF 2002 or Matrimelee. The original two releases remain the places where the company's creative production role can be reconstructed most fully.

A much larger slate that never reached arcades

Two released games can make EVOGA look smaller than the ambitions visible in the surviving material.

Vargas says he began work on Rage of the Dragons 2 and continued on it until EVOGA's end. Later preservation work records concept-art development for the sequel as well. The current evidence supports a real preproduction project, but not a confirmed playable arcade prototype.

A three-on-three basketball game also advanced beyond a vague idea. Vargas remembered a Street Hoop-style concept that would reuse ideas from Evolution Soccer and feature guest KOF characters. Later dossier material describes specific three-player teams and suggests the design reached an advanced conceptual stage. The popular title The King of Basketball remains unverified and should not be treated as EVOGA's confirmed project name.

A platform project called Geometrics is also supported by the preservation dossier, which describes gameplay and mapping work. The later name Geometric's Aztec is not supported by the stronger evidence and should remain rejected unless an original source appears.

Vargas additionally remembered an unnamed shooter and an unnamed side-scrolling adventure project. Other material points to another football concept after Rage of the Dragons. A proposed PlayStation 2 version of Rage also appears in the wider EVOGA record.

These projects belong on the company page because they show that EVOGA was trying to create a second wave of products. They do not belong in the released arcade count.

The projects behind the unreleased slate

EVOGA's abandoned projects survive at different levels of detail. Some are remembered directly by a designer who worked on them. Others are described in a later dossier drawing on staff material and design documents. A project may have had character designs, mapping ideas or a substantial proposal while still lacking a playable program. Those distinctions matter especially for a company whose released catalog is so small: the unpublished slate can easily be made to sound larger and more complete than the evidence allows.

Rage of the Dragons 2 has the strongest firsthand foundation among the original team's unrealized games. Vargas recalled beginning it after working on the first game's North American and Latin American campaign and continuing until EVOGA ended. He also connected the decision to proceed with favorable location-test feedback. The later dossier describes sequel concept-art work after the team returned to Mexico. Together, those accounts establish a genuine follow-up effort. They do not establish a finished game, an executable prototype, a specific hardware target or a reliably scheduled release.

The basketball project is more detailed than a simple entry in a cancellation list. Vargas remembered a Street Hoop-like game that would draw on ideas from Evolution Soccer and include KOF guests. The later dossier describes two three-person teams: Terry, Yuri and Blue Mary on one side, with Brian Battler, Heavy D. and Lucky on the other. Those names reveal how closely the proposal remained connected to the team's SNK relationships. They describe a planned roster, however, rather than a recovered playable selection screen.

The dossier's description of advanced conceptual progress should also be read at that level. Its percentage estimate concerns the concept, not proof that a corresponding percentage of the software had been completed. Character access and licensing negotiations are described in retrospective material, but a signed contract has not been recovered. The proposal can be discussed as a serious design effort without treating the guest characters as an approved commercial license or turning the later label The King of Basketball into an authenticated EVOGA title.

Geometrics represents another direction the company hoped to pursue. The dossier describes a platform-game concept with gameplay and mapping ideas that attracted attention from managers associated with KOF. That gives the project more substance than a name in a modern database. It also identifies the limit: the described design work did not become a released product, and no playable build or settled hardware target is established by the current record. The unsupported Aztec subtitle should not be used to supply missing theme or setting details.

The unnamed shooter and side-scrolling adventure have a more modest evidentiary footprint. Vargas remembered them as early work. Their genres can be retained because they come from his account, but names, settings, platforms and intended dates cannot be supplied from imagination. They show the range of proposals being considered after Evolution Soccer; they do not show that the studio had two additional games close to production.

The later football concept is a separate problem. The dossier describes post-Rage football concept art, while the archived EVOGA website documents Evolution Soccer Club Edition in 2007. Both belong in the company's broader history, but the intervening years and operational collapse prevent an automatic connection. An original design document, an archived announcement explicitly explaining the lineage, or staff testimony linking the projects would be needed to show that the unnamed proposal became Club Edition.

The proposed PlayStation 2 conversion of Rage raises a different kind of boundary. Moving an existing game to a console would have extended the property's audience and the company's business opportunities. The dossier mentions planning or negotiation, but there is no EVOGA PS2 release to add to the catalog. A proposed conversion is neither another original arcade title nor proof that a console-development team had been established. It remains part of the attempted second wave that did not produce a surviving commercial product.

Read together, the slate suggests a company trying to move beyond two initial collaborations into a repeatable development business. That is an interpretation of the varied proposals, not evidence of a single approved production schedule. The planned sequel, sports game, platformer, early genre experiments and possible conversion reveal ambition. Their uneven survival also makes clear why a released-game count and a project inventory must remain separate. EVOGA shipped two arcade games; it considered and worked on more than two ideas.

The abrupt end of the original EVOGA team

The company's endpoint is one of the areas where later summaries create the most confusion.

Many secondary accounts say EVOGA went bankrupt and closed in 2004. No primary bankruptcy or dissolution document has yet been found to prove that statement in legal terms.

Vargas's firsthand account gives a much earlier operational endpoint. He says the EVOGA development operation effectively shut down in mid-June 2002, shortly after Rage of the Dragons reached arcades. The team split up, although some former members continued doing unrelated advertising work from the office until around December.

Ángel Torres's 2016 account points in the same general direction without assigning a legal closing date. He said EVOGA “as such” ended its cycle when sales of Rage of the Dragons and Evolution Soccer were finished. He added that part of the staff continued developing casino and portable-platform games under another corporate name. That firsthand statement is more useful than simply repeating the later phrase “bankrupt in 2004,” because it separates the end of EVOGA's game-development cycle from whatever corporate or IP residue survived afterward.

A later Cultura NeoGeo reconstruction places a broader company close in 2004 and associates former staff with later casino-oriented work, including Fire Token. Because that specific company linkage has not yet been independently verified from a primary corporate or staff source, it is best treated as a lead rather than as a locked successor-company claim.

At first those dates seem irreconcilable. A federal trademark record makes them easier to understand.

In October 2006, Konami Digital Entertainment filed an administrative action seeking lapse of EVOGA's Evolution Soccer trademark. The January 2007 Mexican government notice identifies EVOGA ENTERTAIMENT, S.A. de C.V. as the recorded owner. The cited legal provision concerned non-use of a registered mark during the preceding three years.

That record does not show an active game studio in 2007. It shows that EVOGA's corporate/IP identity remained present in the trademark system after the original development team had collapsed.

The best current chronology is therefore layered:

  • 2000–mid-2002: original EVOGA arcade-development operation.
  • mid/late 2002: development team breaks apart; residual non-game office activity continues briefly.
  • 2004: later sources place the broader business wind-down here, but no primary legal dissolution record is yet in hand.
  • 2006–2007: EVOGA remains the recorded owner of the Evolution Soccer trademark while Konami seeks lapse for alleged non-use.
  • 2007 and later: the EVOGA name reappears publicly, creating a separate continuity question.

It is less tidy than the familiar “2000–2004” shorthand, but it fits the surviving evidence better.

EVOGA came back — at least on the web

The strangest part of the EVOGA chronology is no longer based only on a later wiki claim.

In June 2007, a contemporary report noted that EVOGA's official website had returned with a Coming Soon 2007 message and imagery that appeared to point toward a new Evolution Soccer project.

The archived official site can now be examined directly enough to go further.

The first successful 2007 Wayback capture of the evogae.com homepage is dated June 29, 2007. A July 13 archived rendering exposes the original page's image calls. Among them is a file named:

/images/esce.gif

Initially, that filename was useful but still inferential. A direct browser inspection of the archived asset resolves the ambiguity: the image itself visibly reads “EVOLUTION SOCCER Club Edition.” It shows the familiar ES lettering beneath a soccer ball and laurel motif, with “Club Edition” written below the main title. The archived landing page separately displays “Coming Soon 2007.”

Wayback's CDX index independently confirms that http://www.evogae.com/images/esce.gif was successfully archived on October 19, 2007 as an 83,531-byte GIF.

A historical Neo-Geo.com entry later identifies an image called Evolution soccer club edition.gif and records an announced but cancelled Evolution Soccer Club Edition.

Taken together, the evidence is now direct rather than circumstantial.

Evolution Soccer Club Edition was a real EVOGA-branded 2007 project publicly shown by name on EVOGA's own website.

What the evidence does not prove is equally important. A title graphic and “Coming Soon” page do not establish that a finished arcade build existed, that playable code survived, or even that the project progressed beyond presentation/preproduction material. The evidence also does not show that the original 2000–2002 development team had reconvened, and it does not establish uninterrupted corporate continuity between the original operation and the 2007 revival.

That ambiguity is exactly why the project belongs in the afterlife section of the EVOGA story rather than being quietly inserted next to the two released arcade games.

Arcade Side B evidence box — The 2007 return

The key artifact is no longer a later database claim. EVOGA's archived official site contains a title image that visibly reads EVOLUTION SOCCER Club Edition, while the landing page separately says Coming Soon 2007. That proves the name and the public announcement. It does not prove that a playable arcade build was completed.

Reading the surviving 2007 page

The archival evidence has several separate dates because a website and its component images are not necessarily captured together. The first successful 2007 homepage capture is dated June 29. The July 13 rendering exposes the image path, while the surviving metadata for the title GIF records an October 19 capture. These are dates of preservation, not three independently announced projects or three development milestones. They should not be used to invent a production timetable for Club Edition.

The research project's direct inspection recorded more than a filename. The graphic carried the words Evolution, Soccer and Club Edition, together with a soccer ball, laurel or wreath motif and ES lettering. It also had a purple-and-green glow treatment and Press Start text at the upper corners. Those observations are useful because they identify what was actually visible. The separate Coming Soon message belonged to the landing page. Combining the observations establishes a named public presentation; it does not establish that the graphic was a captured screen from a running game.

The archive index recorded an image/gif response of 83,531 bytes and an archive digest for the asset. Those details identify an archived web image. They are not evidence that the same archive held a game program, a cartridge image or a playable prototype. The distinction is easy to miss when a title graphic looks convincingly like an arcade attract screen. A screen-like design can be promotional material even when no running product has been demonstrated.

The original historical file was verified during research but was not delivered as a raw local asset in the final package. The illustration on this page therefore explains the evidence in a newly made editorial card. Its caption identifies that status. It should never acquire the authority of the missing original simply because both use the same project name. Recovering the archived GIF would improve the documentary presentation; recovering software or a staff account of development would answer a different and larger historical question.

The Mexican trademark record likewise identifies a surviving fragment rather than the whole business. It specifies registration 726211, E S Evolution Soccer y Diseño, and records Konami's application on October 2, 2006. Its January 29, 2007 publication is the notice date, distinct from the filing date and from the original game's release. The notice preserves the recorded corporate owner's name. It does not supply EVOGA's shareholders, its legal incorporation date or the final result of the trademark dispute. Those are still separate missing records.

The brand after the arcade company

The EVOGA name continued to surface after 2007.

The current official EVOGA website is active again in 2026. It says EVOGA is "evolving," that a new site and updated content are being prepared, and that more project news is coming. It links to the @EVOGAE account and carries a current EVOGA Entertainment copyright line.

That is genuine present-day EVOGA-branded activity, but it does not establish that the same legal corporation has operated continuously since 2000.

The distinction is especially important because EVOGA's old intellectual property has also moved independently of the brand. Piko Interactive announced acquisition of Rage of the Dragons rights in 2020, leading eventually to modern rerelease activity outside the original EVOGA development operation.

Modern releases therefore belong in the afterlife of the property, not in EVOGA's original arcade-output count.

What survives today

EVOGA's two released games have very different preservation footprints.

Evolution Soccer survives in emulation and database records, but the Crystal System is far less familiar to collectors than Neo Geo MVS. The current MAME-derived machine record recognizes the game as runnable and identifies the hardware and controls, while still flagging sound emulation as imperfect. That matters because the game can be researched and played today without pretending preservation is technically finished.

Rage of the Dragons benefited from the Neo Geo ecosystem. MVS and AES cartridges, ROM preservation, contemporary credits, soundtrack material and later rereleases have made it much easier to trace than Evolution Soccer. Modern ownership of the property moved beyond the original EVOGA operation, culminating in Piko Interactive's acquisition announcement and later rerelease work.

The imbalance between the two games is itself part of EVOGA's historical problem. The more famous title has a strong preservation afterlife; the first title, which is crucial for understanding how EVOGA actually became a developer, is the one most likely to be reduced to a database line. Arcade Side B should resist that imbalance.

A note on the “first Latin American videogame company” label

Later writing has sometimes called EVOGA the first videogame company in Latin America, and a 2009 UNAM thesis described it as the first Latin American company focused on the videogame industry. That is a useful indication of how EVOGA was remembered in Mexico, but the absolute claim depends heavily on definitions of “videogame company,” “developer,” and earlier electronic-game activity.

For that reason this profile does not use “the first Latin American videogame company” as a settled headline fact. The stronger and less disputable point is already remarkable enough: EVOGA was an early Mexican development company that entered commercial Japanese arcade production, worked directly with Japanese partners, and shipped internationally distributed arcade software.

Why EVOGA belongs on Arcade Side B

EVOGA is easy to underestimate because it does not fit the usual measure of an arcade company.

It did not leave dozens of games. It did not build a famous hardware platform. It did not become a long-lived publisher. Its most important product was released during one of the most unstable periods in the Neo Geo business.

But the company gives us something rarer: a well-documented example of international arcade production from the creative side.

The surviving interviews and credits let us see the pipeline itself.

A Mexican fan/tester circle enters the commercial arcade network through a local distributor. The group is asked to contribute market ideas. Its members travel to Japan. Their role expands into planning and production. A football game is built with BrezzaSoft. A fighting game is designed with Noise Factory. Mexican artists and planners create characters, systems, stages and story concepts; Japanese specialists turn much of that material into Neo Geo code, pixel animation, sound and final gameplay. The company receives visible product credit. Then the business relationship collapses before the planned second wave can become a stable catalog.

That is the overlooked side of EVOGA.

The company was not important because it was secretly large. It was important because, briefly, a small Mexican team gained a genuine seat inside Japanese arcade production — and left enough evidence behind for us to reconstruct how that collaboration actually worked.

The people behind the credit names

The names on EVOGA's games preserve the way the team worked, but they also create a problem for later historians. Many contributors appear under handles rather than the names found in a company register or employment record. Some handles can be connected securely to people through interviews. Others remain credit names whose real identities have not been established. Keeping both kinds visible is more useful than replacing every alias with an attractive but uncertain biography.

The August 2002 EVOGA interview provides an unusually useful period roster. It identifies Angel Sword as director and planner, Mr.Vo as character and graphic designer, and Edu as planner and character designer. Bel-Mont appears in character design and testing, Darb in graphics and demo design, and Hikaru Suf in character design. Orochi and Shin Ryu are listed in testing and demo color work. Vic Dom Cas, Tozani and Eri San appear in backup roles. These are responsibilities given in a contemporary account of the team, rather than reconstructed from a later fan list.

That roster shows that the EVOGA side extended beyond the three-person core Vargas remembered at the transition into full-time work. It also shows why the seven-person HEPTA circle, the surviving early operating core and the staff credited on a released game should not be treated as interchangeable lists. They describe different moments and different kinds of participation. A person can belong to the original circle without holding the same role throughout production; another can contribute to the later game without being one of the original seven.

Ángel Torres and Mario Vargas can be connected firmly to Angel Sword and Mr.Vo. Vargas supplied the identities in his firsthand account, and the released-game credits and contemporary interview agree with their creative leadership roles. José Luis Belmont is similarly supported as Bel-Mont or Bel Mont. Eduardo's connection to Edu and Haruma Edo is supported by converging interviews and credit records. The fuller name Eduardo d' Palma also appears in the later preservation dossier; the identification of the separate director credit Radel remains more provisional.

Erick's connection to Darb is secure at the first-name and handle level. Later researched material supplies Erick Daniel Sánchez as the fuller identity, but that should remain attributed to the later dossier. The same distinction applies to Sergio, called Serg by Vargas and identified more fully as Sergio Pérez or Sergio Pérez Segovia in later material. Valen or Valente remains an unresolved member of the original circle. A missing surname is a gap in the surviving record, not a reason to remove that person from the history.

Some tempting alias matches deserve particular caution. The equation of Zhila with José Luis Belmont is not firmly established, and the Rage credits use Zhila and Bel Mont in different positions. That does not prove they were different people, but it prevents a confident merger. The mappings of Furank or Fukkie to Fabián Roldán and of Vic Dom Cas to Víctor Domínguez remain provisional. Eri San should not be equated with Erick merely because their names seem compatible: the 2002 interview lists Eri San and Darb separately within the same team.

On Evolution Soccer, the mixed staff list is especially revealing. Yosh Jinno appears as executive producer, Radel as director, and Edu as planner. YuritaRo and Hara receive programming credits. Research credits include Zhila, CasDomV and Panda; voice credits include Zhila and Furank. The closing acknowledgment includes the staffs of both EVOGA and BrezzaSoft. It would be misleading to turn every name in that shared production into an EVOGA employee. The credits establish participation and responsibility more securely than they establish the contributor's employer.

Rage of the Dragons likewise places people from both countries inside its production hierarchy. Its directors include Angel Sword, Mamaro and Hidenari Mamoto; the sub-directors include Mr.Vo and Tetsu. Mr.Vo and Edu appear in planning. Mamoto receives the main-programmer credit, with Kazuaki Ezato and Hiroshi Hishikawa as sub-programmers. Character and background credits mix EVOGA-associated contributors with Japanese personnel. The game therefore preserves the collaboration inside its individual credits as well as in the company names attached to it.

Sound and illustration add further layers. Studio Aqua and Toshikazu Tanaka belong to the sound-production side of Rage's record. Bunshichirō Ōma receives an illustration credit. Neither the presence of a memorable illustration nor a name on the soundtrack makes its contributor a member of EVOGA's Mexican staff. Their work belongs in the production history because the game depended on several connected groups, each contributing a different part of the finished result.

The point of retaining these distinctions is recognition. EVOGA's creative work deserves to be visible without absorbing the Japanese programmers, pixel artists, sound contributors and other specialists who helped realize it. Noise Factory's contribution deserves equal precision without reducing the Mexican team to a logo on somebody else's game. The surviving period roster, credits and interviews allow a much more informative account than either shortcut.
