Expanded technical and commercial notes

Sindbad Mystery sits in an unusually interesting technical position inside Sega’s early-1980s arcade catalog. Preservation sources place it on the late Sega G80 Raster platform, but its encrypted main processor and sound arrangement point toward ideas associated with the emerging System 1 era. Current MAME models a Sega 315-5028 encrypted Z80-family main CPU running at roughly 3.87 MHz, with a separate Z80 sound processor at 4 MHz and two SN76496 programmable sound generators. Those figures are useful for understanding the preserved program, but they are not treated as proof that every surviving production board used an identical audio configuration. MAME’s own source comments note evidence for a surviving PCB with different-sounding audio, possibly involving another Sega sound arrangement.

The preservation history is notable in its own right. Sindbad Mystery became a working MAME title in the 0.35b9 period at the end of March 1999, after System 1-style encryption behavior was identified in the G80 Raster driver. The shortname remains sindbadm. Secondary technical references associate the game with board identifier 834-5244, but that number is treated here as a useful identifier rather than a fully documented factory product designation.

Operator configuration adds another layer. Current program and emulation research exposes starting-life choices of three, four, five, or six. The extra-player award can be disabled or set to 10,000, 20,000, or 40,000 points, and the program provides only one such award. MAME source still carries caution around parts of the DIP-switch mapping, so these settings are presented as current program/emulation findings rather than a substitute for an original Sega operator sheet.

The commercial evidence is also more specific than a generic “1983 Sega game” label. Japanese trade research supports a board-set price of ¥132,000. A contemporary Game Machine page shows the game operating in a Japanese table cabinet, which demonstrates a period installation without proving that Sega manufactured a unique dedicated Sindbad Mystery table or upright cabinet. This distinction matters because board-set games were routinely installed into compatible existing furniture, making later cabinet photographs difficult to interpret without provenance.

Period operator ratings offer a rare glimpse at reception: 7.00 on July 15, 1983, followed by 5.85 on August 1, 5.44 on August 15, and 4.88 on September 1. These figures are operator popularity/earnings ratings, not unit sales. They support a picture of a good initial response followed by cooling, but they do not justify calling the game either a major hit or an immediate failure. The frequently repeated 3,000-unit sales figure remains unverified.

Reader snapshot

Released by Sega in Japan in July 1983, Sindbad Mystery is a single-screen treasure-maze game that hides a surprisingly thoughtful puzzle underneath its bright Arabian-fantasy presentation. Thirty question marks gradually reveal a treasure map, but the player is not required to collect them all. Every opened panel earns 100 points; every panel left unopened when the treasure is found is worth 300. The better the player becomes at reading the map, the less information—and risk—he needs.

The game's one action button is unusually versatile. Sindbad can dig holes, fill them back in and shove rocks according to context. Holes temporarily reshape a chase, ordinary enemies can erase them, and rocks can become weapons or hazards depending on elevation and momentum. Stairways, upper walkways, bridges and visually overlapping routes make the maze more three-dimensional than it first appears. After three primary stages, the game loops at higher difficulty, capped by the wonderfully odd sight of Sindbad departing on a flying camel.

Sindbad Mystery also occupies an interesting place in Sega history. It sits at the end of the G80 Raster line while already using hardware ideas associated with the emerging System 1 generation. Period Japanese trade material shows an initially good operator rating that fell over the following weeks, while retrospective accounts connect the game with Steve Hanawa, Yoji Ishii and Yoshiki Kawasaki. Complete original machines remain difficult to document, making surviving Sega flyers, boards, program code and period trade coverage especially important to reconstructing the game accurately.

The map is a wager on how much you know

Thirty question marks make the treasure map look like a conventional collection task. Sega's scoring rule changes the meaning of each mark. Opening one earns 100 points immediately. If Sindbad finds the treasure while a panel is still unopened, that panel is worth 300. For any single clue, the player is effectively exchanging a possible later 300-point reward for a certain 100-point reward and the information needed to find the prize. That is a 200-point difference per panel when the treasure is successfully found, though the comparison does not imply that a player can safely skip every clue.

The mathematical upper bound from thirty unopened panels would be 9,000 points under that particular bonus rule, while opening all thirty would award 3,000 from the panels themselves. Those are illustrations of the rule, not claims about a practical maximum total score or the other events in a round. A blind dig can waste time and expose Sindbad to monsters; a few opened panels may save a life and make the treasure location clear. The game rewards the moment a player decides the evidence is sufficient. On one maze, that point may arrive early. On another, the player may need several more pieces of the map.

This makes the first few question marks different from the last few. Early openings can eliminate wide areas of uncertainty. Once a treasure location is apparent, another clue may contribute little except a smaller immediate award. The player is balancing information, time, danger and points on every route around the maze. The published rule is simple enough to fit on the back of a flyer, but it gives the collection system an unusual degree of judgment.

Koji Kenjoh later remembered the treasure location as changing and recalled that a player might occasionally dig it up with very little map information. That is retrospective player memory, not a contemporary rule sheet. It nevertheless captures the distinctive temptation of the game: act on a hunch before the whole picture is exposed. The precise placement algorithm has not been established, so Kenjoh’s memory should not be presented as though a decoded random-number routine had proved it.

One Button, Several Jobs

Sindbad has only a four-way joystick and one action button, but the button changes function according to what he is facing. ROM analysis found distinct internal actions corresponding to digging, burying/filling and shoving a rock. That economy is important: the game asks the player to understand context rather than memorize a control panel full of commands.

On suitable ground, pressing the button digs a hole. The hole blocks movement and can be used to interfere with a pursuer. Press the button again while properly positioned at one of Sindbad's holes and he fills it back in. This is not an uncertain trick or a later-version feature. Sega's period flyer documents it, the arcade program contains a distinct burying/filling action, and direct MAME play testing confirmed the visible behavior.

Holes are more flexible than the original finished profile suggested. Multiple holes can remain active at once; direct testing maintained at least four consecutive holes until monsters dealt with them. Sindbad can dig on elevated walkways as well as lower ground. He cannot dig directly on stair tiles, and if a monster is already too close he may be unable to start the digging action at all. That last detail matters in practice. A hole works best as a prepared obstacle or as a move made while there is still separation. Waiting until a monster is touching Sindbad is too late.

The monsters also prevent holes from becoming permanent walls. Direct arcade-version testing showed ordinary enemies walking up to holes and removing them. Visually, the hole disappears at roughly the same speed that it appears when Sindbad digs it. Calling this “filling the hole” is a fair description of what the player sees. The enemies are not necessarily trapped forever or forced to make a long detour; they can undo Sindbad's work.

This gives hole placement a useful tactical rhythm. A row of holes can temporarily reshape a chase and buy time, but the player should not assume the route has been permanently sealed. Dig early enough to matter, use the delay to change levels or reach a different corridor, and be ready for the route to reopen behind you.

What direct play reveals about the action button

The period Sega flyer shows the action button doing three different jobs: digging a hole, filling an existing hole and pushing a rock. That is more informative than the loose description “one-button controls.” The button's meaning changes with Sindbad's position and the object immediately in front of him. It asks the player to read the local terrain before acting. A button press near empty ground does not have the same consequence as one beside a rock or an existing hole.

Direct arcade-version testing confirmed that a player-created hole can be filled again. It also maintained at least four holes at once. That last observation establishes a useful lower bound, not an unlimited capacity. A row of holes can interrupt a chase, but ordinary monsters can approach and erase the openings. The player has created a temporary traffic problem rather than permanent construction. If the holes block Sindbad's own intended path, the fill action gives him a way to undo the mistake.

Elevation matters here too. Sindbad can dig on an upper walkway, according to direct testing, but not directly on a stair tile. Nor is a last-second dig guaranteed when an enemy is already close enough to interfere with the action. These limits make preparation more reliable than panic. A player who anticipates where monsters will travel can create space; a player who waits until contact range may never begin the animation.

One small test corrected an intuitive but unnecessary assumption about rocks. A question mark on the far side of a blocking rock can sometimes be uncovered by positioning Sindbad and digging beyond it; he need not always move the rock first. That is exactly the sort of arcade detail that a generalized home-version guide could miss. The distinction arose from direct testing of the arcade game, and it is worth retaining because it changes how a player evaluates a crowded route.

Rocks: Weapon, Tool and Liability

Rocks are the other major system. Sindbad does not carry a conventional weapon. Instead, the action button can shove a rock in the direction he is facing. Once a rock enters free movement, its behavior is governed by its state and the terrain; the normal shove routine does not simply let the player keep re-steering an already autonomous rock.

That distinction matters because the most useful rock play comes from positioning. The player wants to understand where the rock can be sent, where gravity will take over and where enemies are likely to be when it arrives. On ordinary ground a rock can be used to threaten a corridor. On slopes, stairs and dipping bridges, terrain can turn it into a projectile that continues under its own momentum.

Rocks crush enemies, but the scoring system strongly encourages doing more than flattening the first target available. Sega's flyer documents the beginning of the progression, while ROM analysis extends the table: successive enemies hit by the same rock score 200, then 400, then 600, with the sequence reaching 800 points for the fourth and later kills. The practical high-score lesson is obvious. If several monsters can be gathered into the same lane before the rock is sent, the same piece of terrain becomes much more valuable.

The reward comes with several catches. Ordinary monsters eventually return after being crushed, so a successful rock shot does not permanently reduce the population. It gives points and breathing room, not a cleared board. Direct testing also established that rocks are not permanent barriers. When an enemy encounters a blocking rock it may sometimes turn around, but at other times it performs an animation and destroys the rock. In direct arcade-version testing the motion could look like a small cane- or hook-like action; the exact implement is not identified strongly enough to name more specifically. Direct testing also found enemies occasionally removing rocks before Sindbad could even reach them. A player therefore cannot count on leaving a rock in place as a reliable long-term barricade.

Stairs are especially dangerous. A rock that is pushed far enough reaches the landing. Abandon the shove partway up, however, and the rock may sit briefly, shimmying for roughly two seconds, before rolling back down the way it came. That pause gives Sindbad just enough time to turn around, retreat and possibly get around a corner. If he does not, his own rock can crush him.

The returning rock can also crush an enemy. Direct testing proved it. In theory, that makes the rollback an offensive trick: start the rock uphill, abandon it, get clear and let a pursuer take the hit on the return trip. In practice it is a trick shot rather than a dependable strategy. Setting up the enemy, the rock and Sindbad's escape route at the same time is difficult, and the enemies may destroy the rock before the attempt develops. It is worth knowing that the possibility exists, but a new player should treat rollback first as a hazard and only later as something to exploit deliberately.

Rope bridges add another variation. When a rock reaches the appropriate part of a dipping or swinging bridge, it can take off under its own momentum instead of behaving like a rock being continuously shepherded across flat ground. The important lesson is to think of terrain as part of the weapon system. Sindbad does not simply “push rocks”; he places them into situations where slopes and structures determine what happens next.

There is also a useful interaction between rocks and question marks. When a rock blocks Sindbad from standing directly on the far-side marker, he can position himself against the rock and use the action button so the digging action occurs on the opposite side beneath the question mark. This allows the map section to be revealed without first relocating the rock. It is the sort of small mechanical detail that makes Sindbad Mystery considerably more interesting once the player stops treating every obstacle as something that must be moved out of the way.

What direct play reveals about rock behavior

The scoring table makes a well-placed rock attractive. Sega's flyer shows an increasing chain for successive enemies crushed by the same rock, and targeted program analysis extends it to 200, 400, 600 and then an 800-point cap for the fourth and later victims. The reward encourages a player to gather monsters into a shared lane before sending the rock. It is a positioning problem, not a rapid-fire weapon. Ordinary enemies eventually return, so the shot creates points and temporary relief without permanently clearing the level.

Terrain can give the rock its own movement. A shove on level ground, a climb up stairs, and a passage over a dipping bridge do not have identical outcomes. Direct testing found that a rock abandoned partway up a stair route pauses and shimmies for roughly two seconds before rolling back. That delay is a warning, but not a guarantee of safety. Sindbad can be crushed by his own returning rock. An ordinary enemy can also be crushed on the return, which makes a trick shot possible if the positions line up; it is best treated as an advanced opportunity rather than basic advice.

A fully landed uphill rock behaved differently in direct tests and did not produce the same rollback. On some bridges and slopes, momentum carried a rock independently after the initial shove. Once it is freely moving, ordinary pushing input does not let Sindbad steer it like a controllable projectile. The player has to forecast the route before committing. This is why the elevated structures are more than visual variety. They are part of the weapon system.

Rocks are not reliable walls either. A monster may turn away from one, but direct tests also showed enemies destroying a stationary obstruction. The observed animation suggested a small implement, yet its exact identity was not secure enough to name. The safe rule for play is to expect the obstacle to disappear. The safe rule for history is to describe what was seen without building a fictional enemy toolkit around a few pixels.

The skull breaks the ordinary rock logic. The flyer presents it as a special danger, and program analysis found that it does not use the standard rock-crush path. Completing all thirty map panels can release it, but the program also has an earlier activation path that was not translated into a dependable player-facing trigger. The practical lesson is to be ready before the map is complete and not to assume a rock will eliminate this threat. The historical lesson is that code evidence can settle some negative claims while leaving the precise visible rule open.

Reading the treasure map in practice

The treasure map is more than a progress meter. Koji Kenjoh, writing retrospectively from his own experience as a player, describes the treasure position as changing and the map as revealing enough of the field to identify the relevant area. That matches the game's scoring incentives: a confident player can act before every panel is open.

This changes how the question marks should be approached. A beginner naturally wants to collect them all because they look like the obvious objective. That works, and Sega's own current historical description notes that collecting all of them will reveal the treasure path to completion. But efficient play means watching the map after every few panels and asking whether the location is already recognizable.

Because an opened panel is worth 100 points while an unopened panel can be worth 300 at successful completion, every unnecessary clue has an opportunity cost. The game is quietly asking how much certainty the player needs. That is unusually thoughtful design for an arcade title built around short, pressured rounds.

There is also a considerate teaching feature at the other end of the spectrum. Kenjoh recalls that when the game ends before the treasure is found, the completed map can be shown so the player sees where the treasure had been. The effect is almost instructional: the lost credit still teaches the player how the map corresponded to the maze.

The Skull

As the round becomes more dangerous, a large skull joins the hunt. This enemy deserves separate treatment because the program does not handle it as an ordinary monster. The skull is effectively a special pursuer rather than just a tougher member of the existing group.

Earlier versions of this profile said that revealing the final map section directly caused the skull to appear. Period descriptions made the relationship sound more general, as though the skull emerged as play progressed. ROM analysis shows why both descriptions can sound right. Completing all thirty map panels is sufficient to release the skull, but the program also contains a separate path that can activate the skull before the entire map is uncovered.

The precise player-visible condition for that earlier activation has not been established strongly enough to publish as a simple rule. Code analysis found a close relationship between special terrain handling, enemy behavior and the skull's early-activation state, but the final visible trigger is not clean enough to state as “do X and the skull appears.” The useful player-facing advice is simpler and safer: do not assume that leaving one question mark unopened guarantees that the skull will stay away. Finishing the full map releases it, and under some circumstances it can arrive sooner.

Once present, the skull increases the importance of route knowledge. The program does not route the skull through the ordinary rock-crush handling used for the regular enemies, so a rock should not be treated as a way to remove it. Holes and rocks can still affect the shape of the chase, but neither is permanent safety. The layered maze, stairs and escape paths matter more as the pursuit becomes crowded.

Three Islands, Then a Harder Cycle

Sindbad Mystery uses three primary maze designs. Each has its own visual structure and route problems, and the profile's three gameplay images correspond to those three main screens. Clearing a stage leads to a short travel sequence before the next island. One of the period-specific charms is a cameo by Sega's Pengo during the between-island sequence.

After the three-stage group is completed, Sindbad leaves in a much more memorable way. The old profile called his mount a magical horse. It is a camel. The original Sega flyer visibly shows the flying camel, retrospective arcade testimony describes the same scene, and ROM investigation even found an internal music label identifying the “FLYING CAMEL THEME.” The correction is unusually well supported.

After the three stages, the game loops with changed colors and increased challenge. The exact internal difficulty adjustments are less important to the player than the practical result: familiarity with the first three screens is only the beginning. The game expects route knowledge, more efficient treasure deduction and better use of rocks and holes as the cycles continue.

Why the First Credits Can Be Confusing

Sindbad Mystery does not communicate its depth through a complicated control scheme. It communicates it through interactions, and that means a first-time player can easily miss half the game.

Someone approaching it as a Pac-Man-style maze chase may concentrate on collecting every question mark. That player may not understand that the treasure can be found early, that unopened panels are more valuable at completion, that holes can be filled again, that enemies can fill those holes too, that rocks are button-shoved rather than conventional weapons, or that paths crossing visually may be on different levels.

Once those pieces click, the game becomes much more deliberate. The question marks stop being mandatory dots. Rocks stop being scenery. A stairway becomes both a route and a ballistic hazard. A hole becomes temporary control of the maze rather than a permanent wall. The player begins creating situations instead of only fleeing from them.

That is the strongest reason Sindbad Mystery deserves attention. It is not obscure merely because an old Sega logo is attached to it. Its maze contains several systems that reinforce one another, and its scoring encourages the player to understand those systems rather than just survive them.

Hints from the Arcade

Read the maze in three dimensions. Two paths that appear to cross may be on different elevations. Learn which crossings actually connect, where the stairs lead and which routes pass underneath others.

Do not automatically collect every question mark. There are thirty map panels. Each opened panel is worth 100 points, but every unopened panel remaining when the treasure is successfully found is worth 300. Once you recognize the treasure area, consider leaving the remaining clues alone.

Dig before the enemy is on top of you. Direct testing showed that Sindbad can be prevented from starting a hole when a monster is already too close. Use holes proactively rather than waiting until the last instant.

Multiple holes can stay active. At least four simultaneous holes were directly verified. A short row of holes can reshape a chase, but do not assume it will stay that way.

Enemies can fill holes. A pursuer may walk up to one of Sindbad's holes and make it disappear. Use the delay to escape; do not treat the hole as a permanent wall.

Fill your own bad holes. Face an existing hole and use the action button again to fill it. If your own obstacle is blocking the route you now need, undo it.

You can dig on elevated paths, but not on the stairs themselves. That opens more defensive possibilities on the upper sections of the maze than a new player may expect.

Use the button to shove rocks. Rock movement is a context-sensitive action. Once a rock is freely moving, normal re-shoving does not give Sindbad continuous steering control.

Line enemies up before launching a rock. Successive kills by the same rock increase through 200, 400, 600 and then 800 points, with the high end capped at 800 per additional kill. A crowded corridor can be worth far more than an isolated target.

Rock kills are temporary relief. Crushed ordinary enemies return. Take the points and use the opening to reposition.

A rock is not a permanent barricade. Enemies sometimes turn away from a blocking rock, but direct testing also showed them stopping and destroying it. Plan for the route to reopen.

Commit when pushing uphill. If a rock is abandoned partway up the stairs, it pauses and shakes briefly before rolling backward. That warning is usually enough to retreat if you react immediately.

The rollback can kill an enemy, but don't build your whole strategy around it. It works, but setting up the shot deliberately is difficult. Think of it as an advanced trick or lucky opportunity rather than a basic technique.

Use bridge and slope momentum. Terrain can send a rock moving independently. Learn where gravity will take it before pressing the button.

A rock can sometimes be left in place while you uncover a question mark beyond it. Position Sindbad against the rock and use the digging action to reach the marker on the far side rather than assuming the rock must always be moved first.

Do not count on the skull waiting for the last question mark. Completing the thirty-panel map releases it, but ROM analysis indicates that the game can activate it earlier under another condition. Be ready before the round becomes crowded.

Do not expect a rock to eliminate the skull. The skull is handled differently from the ordinary enemies and does not use their normal rock-crush path. Use rocks for space and for ordinary enemies, not as a guaranteed skull weapon.

Development and Sega Context

Sindbad Mystery arrived in July 1983 during a transitional moment for Sega. The company was still producing games associated with its G80 Raster hardware family, but the architecture represented by Sindbad was already moving toward the design choices that would characterize Sega's System 1 generation.

Current MAME documentation describes the earlier G80 Raster platform as a system built around a common CPU/video foundation with game-specific board stacks and Sega's unusual G80 security arrangements. As the hardware evolved, Sega consolidated portions of those stacks. Sindbad sits at the far end of that evolution. MAME's historical notes explicitly describe it as the final G80 Raster game and point out that it uses an encrypted Z80 much like the System 1 games, together with a Z80-driven pair of SN76496 sound chips that closely resemble the later System 1 sound arrangement.

That makes the game's hardware identity slightly more interesting than a simple label such as “Sega G80 Raster.” It belongs to that family, but it also shows Sega already moving away from some of the earlier G80 assumptions.

The development personnel are not presented through an ordinary arcade credit roll, so the safest approach is to combine later interviews and reconstructed credits while distinguishing levels of certainty.

Steve Hanawa is strongly associated with the design and programming. Later credit databases identify him in both roles, and an interview-based account of his Sega career says that Sindbad Mystery was his favorite among the arcade games he programmed because he liked its combination of maze crawling and puzzle solving. That recollection fits the final design remarkably well: Sindbad is recognizably a maze game, yet efficient treasure hunting depends on interpreting information rather than simply clearing the board.

Yoji Ishii's role requires more careful wording. In a 2019 interview, Ishii discussed Sindbad Mystery together with Up'n Down and said that these were projects where plans came down from above and he revised them; he specifically said he was not the director. His later Flicky work was the point at which he described handling a project from zero. The surviving evidence therefore supports crediting Ishii with planning/design involvement or revision of an inherited plan, not as the game’s director.

Yoshiki Kawasaki provides the strongest first-person link to the game's visual production. In Sega's own retrospective interview, Kawasaki recalled working on the arcade Sindbad Mystery and remembered the limitations of the Z80-based board, particularly the small character capacity. Other retrospective credit work connects him with the game's graphics and illustrated material. The important point is not merely to add another name; his recollection helps explain how much visual identity Sega packed into a constrained platform.

No securely documented composer credit was located during this research. That absence is worth preserving rather than filling the gap from assumption or later fan attribution.

That visual identity is easy to underestimate when looking only at screenshots. Sindbad Mystery uses bright sand-colored mazes, water, bridges, stairways, elevated paths, colorful enemies and Arabian-fantasy decoration rather than the black-background geometry common to many maze games of the period. The artwork is not decorative excess. The elevations and structures have to communicate route information, so the graphics are doing mechanical work as well as establishing atmosphere.

Release, Operator Context and Period Reception

Sega's own historical archive dates Sindbad Mystery to July 1983. Period Japanese material promoted it as a puzzle-feeling treasure-hunt video game, and the surviving Sega flyer explains the major systems in unusually useful detail: question marks, digging, filling holes, rock attacks and scoring are all presented as selling points.

Research into Japanese trade material supports a list price of ¥132,000 for the board set. That commercial form matters when discussing cabinet history. A board-set sale could be installed into existing compatible cabinets, and surviving period images do not justify confidently inventing a unique dedicated upright design for Sindbad Mystery.

A contemporary Game Machine advertising page adds one useful piece of physical evidence: Sindbad Mystery is pictured operating in a Japanese table cabinet alongside other products. That photograph verifies a period table installation, but it still does not establish that the pictured cabinet was a unique factory-dedicated Sindbad Mystery model. It is evidence for how the game could be operated, not a license to reconstruct a cabinet design that has not been documented.

The current MAME input definition includes an upright/cocktail cabinet setting, but the same MAME driver still carries a TODO to verify Sindbad Mystery's DIP switches. That setting is therefore technical evidence that cabinet-mode support existed in the program/emulation model, not proof that Sega sold two dedicated cabinet models. Until original cabinet documentation or an unquestionably original survivor is found, the safest publication wording is that the Japanese release is documented as a board-set product and that dedicated cabinet formats remain uncertain.

Game Machine's operator charts provide a useful glimpse of early performance. The July 15, 1983 issue rated Sindbad Mystery 7.00 among new table-type video games. The magazine's own scale described 7 as “good,” based on operators' assessment of popularity and earnings. The subsequent numbers then fell in a clear short sequence: 5.85 on August 1, 5.44 on August 15 and 4.88 on September 1.

That progression is more informative than the later ratings alone. It suggests a game that attracted a genuinely positive initial operator response and then cooled over the following weeks. The figures are not unit sales and should not be turned into a commercial-success chart, but they make two extremes equally misleading: Sindbad Mystery was neither an obvious blockbuster nor a game that arrived to no operator interest at all.

A later online claim that Sega sold 3,000 units has circulated, but no trustworthy primary source for that figure has been located, so it is not treated as fact here.

One of the more interesting later recollections concerns the United States. An interview-based account of Steve Hanawa's Sega career says that after the Japanese release he went to the United States and worked on a localized arcade version. According to that account, the game underwent an “income test,” meaning it was placed in operating locations to measure earnings, and performed reasonably well. The planned release was then overtaken by changes in Sega's American coin-op business. This is valuable context but should remain explicitly attributed to Hanawa's retrospective story rather than presented as if surviving Sega sales paperwork has been found.

A Japanese location-test recollection also survives from Koji Kenjoh, a later game director writing from his own arcade memories. He remembered Sindbad Mystery being tested at KIGAWA in Shinjuku's Kabukicho district, a location strongly associated with Sega location tests. This is retrospective player testimony rather than a surviving Sega test sheet, and Kenjoh is not being credited here as a Sindbad Mystery developer, but the recollection adds useful texture to the game's path from development to release.

Four operator reports, not a sales chart

The surviving Game Machine sequence gives Sindbad Mystery a narrower, better-grounded commercial story than a simple “hit” or “flop.” On July 15, 1983, the new table-video chart gave it 7.00, a value the magazine explained as good on its operator popularity and earnings scale. On August 1 the figure was 5.85; on August 15, 5.44; and on September 1, 4.88. Those dates are issue dates for reports of operator assessment. They are not four shipment totals or a count of cabinets in the field.

The descending sequence suggests early interest followed by cooling over several weeks. It cannot, by itself, tell us whether locations kept the board for months, whether a change in available games displaced it, or how many sets Sega produced. The often-repeated 3,000-unit figure is not supported by a trustworthy period source and therefore remains unverified. A contemporary price of ¥132,000 for a board set tells us about the product on offer, not the number sold.

The same period material pictures the game in a Japanese table cabinet. That is valuable physical evidence of operation, especially for a title whose complete original machines are hard to document. It establishes an installation, not a unique factory furniture model. The program's upright/cocktail setting is another clue about configurations, but current MAME comments still ask for verification of DIP details. The archive record is strongest when each clue does its proper job rather than being stretched into a definitive cabinet catalog.

Hardware and Emulation

Sindbad Mystery is useful for understanding Sega's movement from the G80 Raster era toward System 1. MAME's driver notes make the transition unusually explicit. Earlier G80 CPU boards used Sega security chips that scrambled address lines on writes. Sindbad does not follow that standard arrangement. A note dated March 24, 1999 records the discovery that Sindbad Mystery instead uses Sega System 1-style encryption.

The currently emulated machine configuration uses a Sega 315-5028 encrypted Z80-family main processor, a separate Z80 for sound and two SN76496 programmable sound generators. MAME identifies the game as vertical and maps a four-way joystick plus one action button for each player side.

The sound story is not completely closed. Current MAME comments note that a recording from a surviving Sindbad Mystery PCB sounds different, particularly around the hole-opening and closing effect, suggesting another hardware configuration and possibly Sega's Universal Sound Board. MAME does not treat that alternative as conclusively identified. The technical snapshot should therefore describe the two-SN76496 arrangement as the currently emulated configuration rather than insisting that every production board was identical.

The emulation history has a particularly neat timeline. The MAME source preserves the March 24, 1999 encryption discovery note. Six days later, MAME 0.35b9, released March 30, listed Sindbad Mystery among games newly added or promoted from non-working status. For a game that remained obscure in the wider arcade canon, its emulation story captures a real piece of late-1990s preservation detective work.

Targeted program analysis used the preserved MAME sindbadm set. Program analysis was not treated as a substitute for period documentation. It was used only to answer narrow gameplay questions that the printed sources did not resolve. ROM-derived findings include the thirty-panel structure, scoring tables, distinct context-sensitive button actions, rock/tunnel transport separation, enemy return behavior and internal labels such as the flying-camel music cue.

The ROM itself is not included in the website research record ZIP. It was research evidence, not a redistributable website asset.

People and hardware at a transition

Three retrospective voices illuminate different parts of Sega's work. Steve Hanawa is strongly associated with programming and design and recalled liking this game for its mixture of maze crawling and puzzle solving. Yoji Ishii spoke of revising plans handed down from above and explicitly said he was not the director of projects such as Sindbad Mystery. Yoshiki Kawasaki recalled working on the arcade game under tight character-memory limits before his Flicky work. These accounts identify participation and perspective; they do not recreate a missing contemporary credits screen or identify an unverified composer.

The technical record similarly has an edge of uncertainty. Sindbad belongs to Sega's late G80 Raster line, while its encrypted main processor and sound arrangement point toward the next System 1 generation. A March 1999 MAME note records discovery of the System 1-style encryption, shortly before the game entered working emulation coverage. Current emulation uses a 315-5028 encrypted Z80-family CPU, a separate sound Z80 and two SN76496 chips. A surviving-board recording reportedly differs in some sound effects, however, and MAME comments raise another possible sound configuration. That is a reason to describe the emulated setup precisely, not to assume every original PCB was identical.

There is also a modest player-count conflict. Sega's modern historical archive lists one player, while the arcade program as currently preserved supports two alternating players. Both statements can be true descriptions of their respective sources. The page leaves the discrepancy visible rather than silently replacing Sega's listing or ignoring what the program does.

Versions and Later Appearances

Sindbad Mystery also appeared on Sega's SG-1000/SC-3000 home hardware. The conversion preserves the basic treasure-maze idea but should not be used indiscriminately as proof of arcade behavior. This project deliberately separated arcade evidence from home-version descriptions when a mechanic was in doubt.

The title later appeared in Sega's Saturn-era compilation history through Sega Ages: Memorial Selection Vol. 2 in 1997. Those later appearances are useful evidence that Sega did not entirely forget the game, but this profile remains centered on the 1983 arcade original.

The home version is particularly relevant when evaluating old memories and internet guides. A technique described for SG-1000 or a later compilation may look plausible in the arcade game while behaving differently. That is why questions such as rock transport through tunnels were checked against the arcade program rather than copied from a generalized Sindbad Mystery guide.

Collector's Note

Sindbad Mystery is much easier to encounter today through emulation than as a complete original arcade installation. Surviving original Sega promotional material includes the Japanese flyer, instruction material and board documentation or listings, but complete dedicated machines are difficult to document with confidence.

That scarcity should not be exaggerated into a claim that no original machines survive. Arcade survivor censuses are voluntary, collections change hands privately, and board-set games were especially vulnerable to conversion and cabinet reuse. The careful conclusion is simply that documented complete survivors are uncommon and that loose boards and paper material are more visible in the collector record.

Original instruction cards and working board sets continue to appear in collector markets, showing that parts and paperwork remain in circulation even when complete machines are difficult to document. Isolated auction and asking prices are not treated here as formal market values.

The cabinet question remains the biggest collector caveat. Database images can be dangerously persuasive: one modern Sindbad “cabinet” image circulating in a game-image database is plainly a modern presentation/mockup rather than historical cabinet evidence. It has therefore been excluded from the usable image pack. Until a cabinet photograph can be tied to period Sega documentation or a credible original survivor, the website should resist the temptation to illustrate Sindbad with a made-up dedicated cabinet.

Modern Context

Sindbad Mystery has not become one of Sega's household arcade names, but it has continued to surface among preservationists and serious arcades. Modern arcade evidence also shows Galloping Ghost Arcade using the game as Monday Mystery Game #1102, identifying it as Sega’s 1983 Sindbad Mystery. That modern appearance is best used as a brief closing note rather than as a substitute for the game's period history.

The continued interest makes sense. The game is approachable enough to understand in a credit or two, yet its better ideas are easy to miss without explanation. It is exactly the sort of title that benefits from being profiled rather than merely listed in a database.

Preservation is an ongoing comparison of evidence

Sindbad Mystery's 1999 emulation milestone did not close every technical question. A working program lets researchers inspect control paths and scoring, but a software model can still carry tentative DIP mappings or represent one sound-board configuration more fully than another. A surviving PCB recording that differs from the current emulation is not something to explain away. It is a lead for comparing boards, recordings and schematics when those become available.

The same caution applies to home conversions and later compilations. Sega's SG-1000/SC-3000 version and Saturn-era appearance demonstrate that the title had a life beyond the 1983 arcade board. They do not prove that every familiar trick behaves the same on the original. The project specifically tested the arcade game's hole filling, rock movement and tunnel behavior rather than importing plausible advice from a version with the same name. One discarded tip said rocks travel through tunnels; the arcade program did not support it. Another tentative note about filling holes became a confirmed behavior after direct testing.

This is why a detailed game history benefits from recording corrections, not merely final conclusions. A later reader can see which claim came from Sega's flyer, which came from a first-person recollection, which was observed in play and which required narrow program analysis. Those paths are especially valuable for a board-set game whose cabinet history is incomplete and whose most accessible modern form is emulation.

Why It Is Worth Playing

Sindbad Mystery is worth playing because it does something specific with the maze-game formula. It does not simply give Pac-Man a different theme. The treasure system changes the purpose of collection. The scoring system rewards deduction. The elevations change how the player reads the maze. Holes temporarily reshape routes. Enemies can undo those holes. Rocks create both attacks and hazards, and terrain determines whether a rock is a tool, a projectile or a threat to Sindbad himself.

Most importantly, those systems interact. A player who only runs from monsters will find the game hectic. A player who only collects question marks will miss the scoring logic. A player who treats every rock as a disposable weapon will overlook positioning and terrain. The game gets better as the player understands why each part is there.

It also has personality. The Arabian-fantasy artwork is colorful without overwhelming the playfield. Pengo turns up between islands. Sindbad eventually flies away on a camel. Even the title screen calls the game an “Intelligent Puzzle Game,” a grand little phrase for a design whose real intelligence is that it trusts the player to stop collecting clues once the answer becomes clear.

For an obscure 1983 Sega release, that is plenty of reason to give it another credit.

Sindbad Mystery — Evidence and Source Register

Research record — September 2026

This register preserves the detailed evidence behind the article. Evidence, interpretation, direct verification and unresolved questions are separated so readers can see where each conclusion comes from.

Evidence classes used here

Primary / contemporary: material created by Sega or the amusement trade at or near the time of release.

Primary / first-person retrospective: later statements by people directly involved with Sega or by witnesses describing what they personally did or saw.

Technical preservation evidence: current emulator source, program behavior, targeted ROM analysis and direct gameplay verification.

Secondary / archival: later databases, collector archives and media repositories useful for discovery or corroboration but not treated as equal to primary evidence.

1. Sega 1983 Japanese flyer — PRIMARY / CONTEMPORARY

The surviving visual record includes original Japanese flyer scans plus separate English reader-aid translations. The translations are aids, not original 1983 English Sega material.

What it establishes: - Sega branding and 1983 period identity. - Treasure-hunt / puzzle framing. - The three principal gameplay screens as contemporary promotional imagery. - Context-sensitive button use: digging a hole, filling a hole, and pushing a rock. - 100 points for an opened question-mark/map panel. - 300 points for each unopened panel remaining when the treasure is successfully found. - Rock-chain examples of 200, 400 and 600 points; ROM analysis extends the chain to an 800-point cap for the fourth and later victims. - The skull is a special threat and is described in the translated period instructions as not defeatable. - The flying-camel imagery that corrects the earlier “magical horse” wording. - SG-8305 printed on the instruction/flyer material.

Caution: The English pair is a later reader translation, not an original 1983 English-language Sega flyer. The untouched Japanese scans are the provenance masters.

7. Targeted ROM analysis — TECHNICAL PRESERVATION / DIRECT EVIDENCE

ROM binaries are not redistributed here.

Questions targeted and conclusions preserved: - 30 treasure-map panels. - Question panel 100 points; unopened panel at successful treasure 300 points. - Rock-chain sequence 200, 400, 600, then 800-point cap for fourth and later victims. - One context-sensitive action system with distinct digging, burying/filling and rock-shoving paths. - Extra-player program/DIP options: disabled, 10,000, 20,000 or 40,000 points; one extra-player award. - Starting lives options 3/4/5/6. - Rocks do not use Sindbad's tunnel/warp transport routine. - The skull is a special object/enemy path and does not use the ordinary rock-crush routine. - Completing all 30 map panels is sufficient to activate/release the skull, but another program path allows earlier activation; exact player-visible condition remains unresolved. - Internal “FLYING CAMEL THEME” label corroborates the flyer/retrospective identification of the end-of-cycle mount.

Scope: These are targeted conclusions from preserved program material, not a claim of exhaustive reverse engineering.

8. Direct arcade-version gameplay verification — FIRSTHAND TESTING

Directly verified in arcade-version testing: - A player-created hole can be filled again. - At least four holes can exist simultaneously; do not convert this into an unsupported “unlimited holes” claim. - Sindbad can dig on elevated upper paths but not on stair tiles themselves. - A nearby enemy can prevent initiation of a dig. - Ordinary enemies can approach and eliminate/fill holes. - Ordinary enemies can destroy a stationary/blocking rock; behavior can include a cane/hook-like animation, but the implement should not be over-identified. - An uphill rock abandoned before fully landing pauses/shimmies for roughly two seconds before rolling back. - The rollback can crush Sindbad or an ordinary enemy. - A fully landed uphill rock avoids the rollback. - Bridge/slope terrain can give rocks autonomous momentum. - A question mark beyond a blocking rock can be uncovered by digging on the far side without first moving the rock.

Corrections from direct verification: - “Rocks may travel through tunnels” — FALSE. Remove. - “Hole filling may be possible” — CONFIRMED. No longer tentative. - “Magical horse” — FALSE. It is a flying camel.
